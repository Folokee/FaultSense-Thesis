\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:pv-introduction}

This chapter presents the generalities of photovoltaic systems, including their operating principles, main components, and monitoring infrastructure, followed by a description of the most common faults that can affect PV installations. The final part provides a background on artificial intelligence for PV Fault Detection and Diagnosis (FDD), with a focus on the machine learning and deep learning methods that are relevant to detection and diagnosis tasks and that are used throughout this work.

\section{Fundamentals of Photovoltaic Systems}
\label{sec:pv-fundamentals}

A photovoltaic (PV) system directly converts solar radiation into electricity via the photovoltaic effect. It primarily comprises PV modules to capture sunlight, inverters to convert direct current to alternating current, and protection devices for safe operation, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pv-system}. Ranging from residential rooftops to utility-scale farms, a PV system's performance is driven by solar irradiance, ambient temperature, load characteristics, and cell technology \citep{alezz2022photovoltaic}.

\subsection{The Photovoltaic Effect}
\label{sec:pv-effect}

The photovoltaic effect, first observed by Edmond Becquerel in 1839, occurs when absorbed photons generate electron--hole pairs in a semiconductor p--n junction \citep{wurfel2016physics}. In silicon solar cells, this junction is formed by joining n-type and p-type regions, creating an internal electric field that separates photogenerated carriers and produces an external current under illumination \citep{marques2022photovoltaic}. Figure~\ref{fig:pv-effect} illustrates this principle \citep{energyeducation_pveffect}. The illuminated cell can be modeled by:
\begin{equation}
I = I_{\text{L}} - I_{0} \left[ \exp\left( \frac{q(V + I R_{\text{s}})}{n k_{\text{B}} T} \right) - 1 \right] - \frac{V + I R_{\text{s}}}{R_{\text{sh}}}
\label{eq:single-diode}
\end{equation}
where $I_{\text{L}}$ is the photocurrent, $I_{0}$ the dark saturation current, $n$ the ideality factor, $k_{\text{B}}$ the Boltzmann constant, $T$ the absolute temperature, $q$ the elementary charge, and $R_{\text{s}}$ and $R_{\text{sh}}$ the series and shunt resistances. This model is widely used in photovoltaic research because its five parameters ($I_{\text{L}}$, $I_{0}$, $n$, $R_{\text{s}}$, $R_{\text{sh}}$) can be extracted from measured I--V curves and serve as indicators of cell health and degradation \citep{alezz2022photovoltaic}.

\subsection{Components of a Photovoltaic System}
\label{sec:pv-components}

A photovoltaic system comprises several functional components designed to capture, regulate, convert, and distribute electrical energy safely \citep{franklinSolarPhotovoltaicPV}. The main components are detailed below.

\textbf{Solar Modules and Arrays:} The solar module is the foundational element that captures sunlight using multiple interconnected mono-crystalline or poly-crystalline silicon cells. To meet higher voltage and power requirements, several modules are wired together in series and parallel configurations to form a larger solar array \citep{doe2019pvcells101}. Figure~\ref{fig:pv-components} illustrates this hierarchy.

\end{figure}

\textbf{Combiner Box:} This electrical enclosure aggregates multiple series strings of solar modules into a single parallel output circuit, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:combiner-box}. By consolidating these connections, the combiner box efficiently increases the total system current while maintaining the accumulated voltage of the strings.

\begin{figure}[H]

\textbf{Charge Controller:} Positioned between the solar array and the battery bank, the charge controller regulates the incoming direct current (Figure~\ref{fig:charge-controller}). It plays a critical role in preventing battery overcharging and excessive discharging, thereby safely extending the operational lifespan of the storage system.

\textbf{Battery Bank:} In systems requiring energy storage, deep-cycle lead-acid batteries are utilized to store the electrical power produced during sunny periods. These batteries provide a reliable energy reserve to continuously power electrical loads during the night or periods of low solar irradiance.

\textbf{Inverter:} The inverter is a crucial power electronics device that converts the direct current generated by the array or stored in batteries into alternating current (AC), shown in Figure~\ref{fig:inverter}. This conversion is essential because most residential and commercial electrical loads, as well as the external utility grid, operate exclusively on alternating current.

\textbf{Disconnect Switches and Breakers:} Direct and alternating current disconnect switches are installed to safely isolate the power sources and the inverter during routine maintenance. These safety mechanisms ensure that the system can be completely de-energized, protecting the functional equipment and the maintenance personnel.

\subsection{Surveillance Systems}
\label{sec:pv-surveillance}
In the PV context, surveillance systems refer to the integrated hardware and software tools used to collect and monitor system data, including electrical and meteorological parameters. These systems are generally composed of sensors and a Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) platform that collects, processes, and visualizes the measurements for performance monitoring and fault detection \citep{taylorEffectivenessSCADASystem2020}.

A SCADA system typically includes field devices (sensors and actuators). In a PV plant, the main sensors include a pyranometer to measure solar irradiance, a thermometer for ambient temperature, and an anemometer for wind speed measurement. Electrical quantities such as voltage, current, and power are measured at the inverter level. Remote Terminal Units (RTUs) gather the signals from sensors, while Master Terminal Units (MTUs) act as central nodes that collect data from RTUs and provide interfaces for human operators. The collected data is also stored in a database for historical analysis \citep{yadavArchitectureSecuritySCADA2021}. An example of a SCADA system for PV monitoring is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:scada-pv} \citep{syamsuddinPredictiveMaintenanceBased2024}.

\subsection{Global Operation of a Photovoltaic System}
\label{sec:pv-global-operation}

The operation of a photovoltaic system begins with the conversion of solar irradiance into 
direct current at the cell and module levels. Modules are interconnected into strings and 
arrays to achieve the required voltage and power levels. The generated DC power passes 
through protection and aggregation devices, and may be regulated by a charge controller 
when energy storage is used. An inverter then converts the DC power into alternating 
current for AC loads or grid injection. System operation is continuously monitored 
through sensors and SCADA platforms, which collect meteorological and electrical
measurements for performance supervision and fault detection. Figure~\ref{fig:pv-global-operation} summarises this end-to-end operation chain.

\section{Faults in PV Systems}
\label{sec:pv-faults}

Faults in a photovoltaic systems are any abnormal condition or physical damage causing operational parameters
to deviate from their normal range, leading to reduced performance, safety hazards, or system failure.
These anomalies can be grouped into different categories based on their different characteristics. \citep{elbanbyPhotovoltaicSystemFault2023} classified them into infant failures, mid-life 
failures and wear-out failures. While \citep{ArtificialIntelligenceInternet2021} categorized them into temporal and permanent faults. \citep{ComparativeAnalysisSolar2022} divided them into AC side 
faults and DC side faults. Down below are the most pertinent faults that can occur in a PV system:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Open Circuit Fault:} Caused by a break in electrical continuity due to damaged cables, connectors, or fuses, leading to loss of power generation in the affected string.

    \item \textbf{Short Circuit Fault:} Occurs when unintended low-impedance paths form between conductors or to ground, producing abnormal current flow and safety risks.

    \item \textbf{Mismatch Fault:} Results from differences in module electrical characteristics caused by defects, degradation, or non-uniform operating conditions such as partial shading.

    \item \textbf{Hotspot Fault:} Appears when a cell operates in reverse bias and dissipates energy as heat, potentially causing permanent thermal damage.

    \item \textbf{Bypass Diode Fault:} Arises when a bypass diode fails, either reducing module output in short-circuit mode or increasing hotspot risk in open-circuit mode.

    \item \textbf{Shading Fault:} Produced by partial obstruction of sunlight, creating current imbalance between illuminated and shaded cells.

    \item \textbf{Soiling Fault:} Caused by dust or dirt accumulation on module surfaces, reducing irradiance absorption and power generation.

    \item \textbf{Thermal Stress Fault:} Results from prolonged high temperatures that accelerate aging and degradation of module materials and interconnections.

    \item \textbf{Inverter Fault:} Occurs when the power conversion stage malfunctions, affecting DC/AC conversion, cooling, or maximum power point tracking.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical Background of Relevant AI Methods}
\label{sec:ai-fdd}

Artificial intelligence (AI) techniques are increasingly adopted to support automated monitoring of photovoltaic systems by extracting diagnostic information from heterogeneous measurements, including electrical signals, meteorological variables, thermographic imagery, and I--V characteristics. In the context of Fault Detection and Diagnosis (FDD), AI-based methods typically address two related problems: fault detection, which aims to identify deviations from normal operation, and fault diagnosis, which aims to determine the fault type, severity, or location once an anomaly is detected. Survey studies report that data-driven approaches can complement classical threshold-based rules by learning complex nonlinear relationships and by adapting to installation-specific behavior \citep{mellit2018artificial,kurukuru2021machine}.


\subsection{Online Methods}
\label{sec:online-methods}

Online methods are designed to operate on streaming data, processing one observation at a time and maintaining a compact internal state that summarises the relevant past. They are particularly attractive for PV monitoring because they can run causally on edge hardware without storing or re-scanning large historical buffers. Examples include sequential change-detection procedures such as the Page-Hinkley test and the Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) chart \citep{page1954continuous}, as well as probabilistic frameworks like Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection (BOCD) that maintain a full posterior over regime changes \citep{BayesianOnlineChangepoint2007}.

\subsection{Offline Methods}
\label{sec:offline-methods}

Offline methods are trained on a fixed historical dataset and deployed for inference without further parameter updates. They constitute the majority of approaches explored in PV fault detection and diagnosis research, ranging from classical machine learning models that operate on hand-crafted features to deep learning architectures that learn hierarchical representations directly from data.

\subsubsection{Machine Learning Methods}
\label{sec:offline-ml}

Machine learning (ML) methods for PV FDD typically rely on engineered features extracted from electrical and meteorological measurements. These methods are generally computationally efficient, interpretable, and well-suited to tabular data, making them a practical choice for deployment in resource-constrained monitoring environments.

\paragraph{Kernel Methods.}
Kernel methods map input data into a higher-dimensional feature space where linear separation becomes possible, using a kernel function to compute inner products without explicitly constructing the high-dimensional representation \citep{vapnik1995nature}. This approach is particularly effective when the decision boundary between normal and faulty operation is nonlinear in the original measurement space.

Support Vector Machines (SVM) are supervised learning algorithms designed for classification and regression tasks. The main objective of SVM is to determine an optimal separating hyperplane that maximizes the margin between different classes in the feature space. For nonlinear problems, kernel functions are used to project the data into a higher-dimensional space where linear separation becomes possible.

Given a training dataset $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, the decision function is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
f(x)=\text{sign}(w^Tx+b),
\end{equation}
where $w$ is the normal vector of the hyperplane and $b$ is the bias term. For nonlinear classification, kernel functions such as the radial basis function (RBF) are commonly employed:
\begin{equation}
K(x_i,x_j)=\exp\bigl(-\gamma \lVert x_i-x_j \rVert^2\bigr).
\end{equation}

The One-Class SVM (OC-SVM) variant extends this principle to semi-supervised anomaly detection by learning the smallest enclosing region of normal data in feature space \citep{scholkopf2001oneclass}. SVM models are widely used in PV fault diagnosis because of their strong generalization capability and robustness in high-dimensional feature spaces.

\paragraph{Ensemble Methods.}
Ensemble methods combine multiple base learners to produce a single prediction that is more robust and accurate than any individual model. In PV fault diagnosis, ensemble techniques are valued for their ability to handle noisy sensor data, capture complex feature interactions, and maintain calibration under class imbalance through native weighting mechanisms.

\subparagraph{Decision Trees}

Decision Trees (DT) are hierarchical supervised learning models that recursively partition the feature space into subsets according to decision rules. Each internal node represents a condition on an input feature, while leaf nodes correspond to output classes.

The selection of the optimal split is generally based on impurity measures such as entropy:
\begin{equation}
H(S)=-\sum_{i=1}^{C}p_i\log_2(p_i),
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ is the probability of class $i$ and $C$ is the number of classes.

Decision Trees are interpretable and computationally efficient, making them suitable for fault classification and rule-based diagnostic systems. An unsupervised variant, the Isolation Forest (IF), detects anomalies by constructing an ensemble of randomized trees and exploiting the property that outliers are isolated in fewer splits than normal observations \citep{liu2008isolation}; it outputs a directly thresholdable anomaly score without requiring labeled fault data.

\subparagraph{Random Forest}

Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning method that constructs a collection of $B$ decision trees and aggregates their predictions \citep{breiman2001random}. For classification, the forest output is determined by majority voting over the individual tree predictions $\hat{y}_b(x)$:

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}(x) = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{1}\{\hat{y}_b(x) = c\},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of fault classes. Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample drawn with replacement from the training data, and at each split only a random subset of $m = \lfloor\sqrt{p}\rfloor$ features (where $p$ is the total number of features) is considered, introducing controlled diversity across the ensemble. The combination of bootstrap aggregation and random feature selection reduces variance and guards against overfitting, particularly in high-dimensional settings where individual decision trees are prone to memorising noise.

A distinctive advantage of Random Forests is the out-of-bag (OOB) error estimate, which provides an unbiased performance evaluation without requiring a separate validation set. For each training sample $(x_i, y_i)$, the OOB prediction is obtained by aggregating only the trees for which that sample was not included in the bootstrap draw:

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{\text{OOB}}(x_i) = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \sum_{b \,:\, x_i \notin \mathcal{D}_b} \mathbb{1}\{\hat{y}_b(x_i) = c\},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{D}_b$ is the bootstrap training set of tree $b$. The OOB error is then the fraction of incorrectly classified OOB samples. Random Forests output native feature importance measures and are robust to irrelevant predictors, making them suitable for PV fault diagnosis tasks that involve many candidate features whose individual contributions require assessment. A related variant, Extremely Randomized Trees (Extra Trees), carries randomisation further by also selecting split thresholds uniformly at random rather than optimising them, yielding lower variance at the cost of slightly higher bias \citep{geurts2006extremely}.

\subsubsection{Deep Learning Methods}
\label{sec:offline-dl}

Deep learning (DL) methods learn hierarchical feature representations directly from raw or minimally processed data, reducing the dependence on manual feature engineering. In PV fault detection and diagnosis, DL architectures are applied to time-series measurements, thermographic imagery, and I--V curve data, and they have demonstrated strong performance when sufficient training data is available.

\paragraph{Sequence Modeling.}
Sequence modeling methods are designed to capture temporal dependencies in ordered data. In the PV context, electrical and meteorological measurements form multivariate time series where the history of past observations informs the interpretation of the current state, making sequence-aware architectures a natural fit.

\subparagraph{Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN)}

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are a class of neural architectures designed to process sequential data by maintaining a hidden state that captures information from past time steps. At each step $t$, the hidden state $h_t$ is updated as a function of the current input $x_t$ and the previous state $h_{t-1}$, enabling the network to model temporal dependencies across variable-length sequences. Standard RNNs suffer from the vanishing gradient problem, which limits their ability to capture long-range dependencies.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks address this limitation by introducing memory cells and gating mechanisms that control the flow of information \citep{hochreiter1997lstm}.

The forget gate is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
f_t=\sigma(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_f),
\end{equation}
while the cell state update is:
\begin{equation}
C_t=f_tC_{t-1}+i_t\tilde{C}_t.
\end{equation}

LSTM networks are widely applied in PV fault diagnosis because they effectively capture temporal variations in voltage, current, irradiance, and temperature signals.

\subparagraph{State-Space Models (SSM)}
\label{sec:bg-mamba}

State-space models (SSMs) are a class of sequence models that represent dynamical systems through a latent state evolving over time according to linear differential equations. Classical SSMs, such as the Structured State-Space Sequence model (S4) and its efficient diagonal variant (S4D), use fixed transition matrices to propagate information across time steps with linear computational complexity \citep{gu2022s4}, offering a compelling alternative to attention-based architectures for long-range sequence modeling. However, their static parameterisation limits the ability to selectively focus on relevant portions of the input sequence.

A continuous-time SSM maps an input signal $x(t) \in \mathbb{R}$ to an output $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}$ through a latent state $h(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$:
\begin{align}
h'(t) &= \mathbf{A}\,h(t) + \mathbf{B}\,x(t), \label{eq:ssm-cont}\\
y(t)  &= \mathbf{C}\,h(t),
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is the state-transition matrix, $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1}$ the input projection, and $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N}$ the output projection. For discrete sequence processing, the continuous system is discretised with a time-step $\Delta$ via the zero-order hold rule:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{A}} = e^{\Delta \mathbf{A}}, \qquad
\bar{\mathbf{B}} = (\Delta \mathbf{A})^{-1}\!\left(e^{\Delta \mathbf{A}} - \mathbf{I}\right)\Delta \mathbf{B},
\end{equation}
yielding the recurrence $h_t = \bar{\mathbf{A}}\,h_{t-1} + \bar{\mathbf{B}}\,x_t$, $\; y_t = \mathbf{C}\,h_t$.

Mamba addresses the static-parameterisation limitation through an input-dependent selection mechanism that enables the model to selectively retain or discard information along the sequence \citep{gu2023mamba}. The matrices $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{C}$, and the step size $\Delta$ become functions of the current input:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B}_t = s_B(x_t),\quad \mathbf{C}_t = s_C(x_t),\quad \Delta_t = \mathrm{softplus}\!\left(s_\Delta(x_t)\right),
\end{equation}
where $s_B$, $s_C$, and $s_\Delta$ are learned linear projections. This selective parameterisation captures long-range dependencies while maintaining constant memory and compute per time step, making it attractive for deployment on edge hardware where quadratic attention complexity is prohibitive.

\subparagraph{Transformer}

Transformer models are deep learning architectures based entirely on attention mechanisms rather than recurrent structures \citep{vaswani2017attention}. Their main advantage lies in the ability to capture long-range dependencies while enabling parallel computation.

The self-attention mechanism is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
\end{equation}
where $Q$, $K$, and $V$ denote the query, key, and value matrices.

Transformers are increasingly used in PV fault diagnosis and forecasting due to their strong sequence modeling capability.

\paragraph{Reconstruction-Based Methods.}
Reconstruction-based detectors learn to reproduce normal operating data and flag deviations from this learned representation as anomalies. They are trained exclusively on normal-class samples, making them well-suited to semi-supervised anomaly detection in settings where fault-labeled data is scarce.

Autoencoders (AE) are unsupervised neural networks designed to learn compressed latent representations of input data. An autoencoder consists of an encoder function that maps the input into a latent space and a decoder function that reconstructs the original input.

The latent representation is obtained as:
\begin{equation}
z=f(Wx+b),
\end{equation}
while the reconstructed output is:
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}=g(W'z+b').
\end{equation}

The network is trained by minimizing the reconstruction error:
\begin{equation}
L=\lVert x-\hat{x} \rVert^2.
\end{equation}

Variational Autoencoders (VAE) extend conventional autoencoders by learning probabilistic latent distributions instead of deterministic representations \citep{kingma2014vae}. Rather than encoding a single latent vector, the encoder estimates the mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ of a probability distribution:
\begin{equation}
z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2).
\end{equation}

VAEs improve latent space continuity and are suitable for anomaly detection and generative modeling.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:ch1-conclusion}

Photovoltaic installations are complex engineered systems that combine operational components for energy conversion and conditioning with protection devices for safe operation and monitoring infrastructures for supervision. This complexity is reflected in the diversity of signals and subsystems involved, ranging from PV modules and power electronics to disconnect devices, sensors, and SCADA platforms responsible for data acquisition and visualization.

Because PV plants operate under variable environmental conditions and rely on many interconnected components, they remain vulnerable to a wide range of faults that can progressively degrade performance or create safety risks if they are not handled promptly. Maintaining energy yield and ensuring system longevity therefore requires timely fault detection and diagnosis, ideally based on continuous monitoring data. In this context, different families of AI methods provide a compelling solution, since machine learning and deep learning models can exploit the available measurements to detect abnormal behavior and support diagnostic decisions at scale.
