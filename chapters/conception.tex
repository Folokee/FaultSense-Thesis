\section{Introduction}

This chapter specifies the design of the proposed photovoltaic Fault Detection and Diagnosis (FDD) system. It introduces the overall architecture and motivates the choice of field datasets used for development and evaluation. Based on exploratory analyses, it then defines a staged, reproducible pipeline (ingestion, splitting, preprocessing, feature engineering, and training) together with leakage-prevention safeguards, calibration policies, and the evaluation methodology used throughout the experimental chapters.

\section{System Architecture}
The overall architecture includes 4 main components, as illustrated in figure \ref{fig:system_architecture}:
\par\noindent\textbf{Data collection and transformation:} Collects raw data from sensors such as irradiance and ambient temperature, and electrical signals and applies the necessary preprocessing and transformations
to make it suitable for input to the next stage of the system.
\par\noindent\textbf{Anomaly Detection:} Upon receiving the preprocessed data, this component detects persistent deviations from expected values under observed meteorological conditions. Such deviations may indicate an anomaly in the system.
\par\noindent\textbf{Fault Diagnosis:} If an anomaly is detected, this component's responsibility is to determine the type of fault that has occurred from a pre-determined set of faults.
\par\noindent\textbf{Monitoring Interface:} Displays the system's real-time status, including detected anomalies, diagnosed faults along with their confidence scores, incoming data visualizations and performance reports.

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}
\label{sec:eda}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The exploratory data analysis was structured as a series of checks applied to the Costa dataset before any modeling decision was made. Each check follows the same protocol: define the check, run it, record the finding, and record the explicit downstream consequence for the preprocessing or feature engineering described in Sections~\ref{sec:preprocessing} and~\ref{sec:feature-engineering}. This traceable structure is the basis for claiming that every pipeline design choice is data-driven rather than assumed.

\textbf{Schema validation and basic statistics.} The first check confirmed data types, row counts, and channel completeness. Costa yields 515,958 daytime rows over 9 sensor channels (\texttt{vdc1}, \texttt{vdc2}, \texttt{idc1}, \texttt{idc2}, \texttt{pdc1}, \texttt{pdc2}, \texttt{pdc}, \texttt{irr}, \texttt{pvt}), with 0 missing values after ingestion-time irradiance trimming. The dataset exposes all labeled classes without gaps in the label column.

\textbf{Temporal coverage and sampling regularity.} The distribution of consecutive timestamp differences was computed over the full recording; gaps were catalogued at thresholds of 30\,s, 60\,s, and 300\,s. For Costa, p50 = p90 = p99 = 1.0\,s: the recording is a near-perfect 1~Hz sequence with only 20 consecutive pairs exceeding 30~s. This near-perfect regularity means that no imputation step is required and that the 300-second gap threshold for episode segmentation (Section~\ref{sec:splitting}) is a very conservative boundary. The sampling interval distribution is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:eda-sampling}.

\end{figure}

\textbf{Missing value analysis.} Costa contains 0 missing values across all 9 channels after ingestion-time irradiance trimming. The missing-value analysis therefore required no intervention for this dataset.

\textbf{Target distribution and fault temporal structure.} The class distribution was examined at 2 levels of granularity: row count and episode count. Figure~\ref{fig:eda-imbalance} shows the row-share versus episode-share for Costa. At the row level, the normal class accounts for 59.9\% and the naturally-occurring shadowing class (label~4) for 35.7\%; the 3 electrically induced fault classes together account for 4.4\%. At the episode level, shadowing occupies 48.9\% of episodes despite its 35.7\% row share, because shadowing episodes are numerous but short (p90 duration $\approx$~91~s), while induced fault episodes are few but long (median $\approx$~590~s for short circuit and open circuit).

\textbf{Univariate distributions: normal versus fault.} Mann-Whitney U tests were applied in 2 configurations, binary (normal versus all faults pooled) and per-class (normal versus each fault class individually), with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction applied to all $p$-values. Figure~\ref{fig:eda-boxplots} shows the distributional contrast for the highest-ranked channels. All 9 channels produced $p$-values at the numerical floor of zero in both configurations, with FDR correction confirmed for every class combination. The per-class rank-biserial correlations reveal qualitatively distinct signatures: short circuit (label~1) depresses vdc2 toward near-total voltage collapse ($r_{\mathrm{rb}}$ approaching $-1.00$) while elevating idc1; open circuit (label~3) collapses both strings' voltage and current simultaneously (vdc1 at $r_{\mathrm{rb}} = -0.73$); shadowing (label~4) uniformly suppresses current and power in both strings with no corresponding voltage elevation ($r_{\mathrm{rb}}$ between $-0.64$ and $-0.79$). Kruskal-Wallis effect sizes range from $\varepsilon^2 = 0.14$ (vdc2) to $\varepsilon^2 = 0.45$ (idc2), indicating that class membership alone explains up to 45\% of the variance in the DC current channels.

\textbf{Correlation, collinearity, and spurious relationship detection.} 
Spearman rank correlation was computed in 3 contexts: across all rows, restricted to normal-class rows, and stratified by irradiance quartile within the normal class. 
Figure~\ref{fig:eda-correlation-heatmap} shows the full normal-class Spearman heatmap and Figure~\ref{fig:eda-correlation-stratified} shows the irradiance-stratified profiles. 
The normal-class context exposes 2 classes of alias. 
The first class arises from additive identities: Spearman $\rho$(pdc1, pdc) = 0.999 and $\rho$(pdc2, pdc) = 0.999, empirically confirming $P_{\mathrm{dc}} = P_{\mathrm{dc},1} + P_{\mathrm{dc},2}$. 
The second class arises from the multiplicative physical relationship $P_{\mathrm{dc},s} = V_{\mathrm{dc},s} \cdot I_{\mathrm{dc},s}$ at approximately constant voltage, making per-string current a near-proxy for per-string power ($\rho = 0.976$ for idc1--pdc1). 
VIF quantifies the regression-based consequence: VIF(\texttt{idc1}) = 187.8. 
These aliases are the direct targets of the hygiene pruning stage in Section~\ref{sec:feature-engineering}. 
The irradiance-stratified analysis reveals an additional, non-trivial pattern: while the pdc1--pdc2 correlation remains above 0.95 across all 4 irradiance quartiles, the idc1--idc2 correlation drops to 0.82 in the third quartile (830--885~W/m$^2$) before recovering at peak irradiance. 
This regime-dependent inter-string divergence is invisible in an aggregate correlation and is preserved as evidence of non-uniform string behavior under high thermal loading, motivating the per-string imbalance features in Section~\ref{sec:feature-engineering}. 
The aggregate heatmap identifies the algebraic aliases targeted by hygiene pruning, while the irradiance-stratified profiles distinguish those aliases from physically meaningful inter-string divergence that must be preserved.

Multi-class MI is consistently and substantially higher than binary MI across every channel: pdc2 increases from 0.275 to 0.425 bits (a 54\% gap), and idc2 from 0.270 to 0.417 bits. \textbf{Outlier analysis.} A $3\times\mathrm{IQR}$ criterion was applied exclusively to normal-class rows; bounds were computed from the normal-class IQR and any row falling outside those bounds was flagged. The result is strongly channel-asymmetric: idc1, idc2, pdc1, pdc2, pdc, irr, and pvt exhibit 0 outlier rows, while vdc1 yields 1.4\% (4,430 rows). Localizing these vdc1 outliers by date and by hour of day reveals that they are concentrated in the first 3 recording days (2020-04-06 to 2020-04-09) within the 9--12~UTC morning window, a pattern consistent with system warm-up behavior at the start of the recording. Fault-labeled rows that exceed the same vdc1 bounds are not removed; their extreme voltage values are meaningful fault signatures. This label-aware treatment is the direct specification for the IQR-based preprocessing in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\textbf{Sensor health: stuck-at detection.} A rolling variance check was applied across all channels: no 30-sample window exhibits zero variance for any electrical channel. The ingested data is free of frozen-sensor artifacts and is clean on this criterion.

\section{Pipeline Architecture}
\label{sec:pipeline-architecture}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The data pipeline that transforms raw field recordings into the inference-ready representations consumed by every modeling experiment is organized as 5 sequential stages: ingestion, splitting, preprocessing, feature engineering, and model training. This staged organization is a deliberate architectural choice rather than an implementation convenience. A monolithic transformation script would make it impossible to enforce leakage-prevention invariants at a well-defined boundary, to rebuild expensive downstream stages independently when upstream logic changes, or to apply the same pipeline to a new field dataset without touching the transformation code. The staged structure addresses all 3 requirements simultaneously and is the structural foundation of the portability claim: the same pipeline code, parameterized by a site-specific configuration file, produces independent artifact trees for each field dataset with no shared internal state.

The pipeline is constructed as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of processing stages. Each stage defines formal inputs and outputs, ensuring that downstream processes strictly depend on verified upstream states. This structural abstraction enforces data lineage and prevents the reuse of stale data when upstream logic changes, ensuring end-to-end reproducibility independently of the underlying execution engine. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the complete pipeline and the role of each stage.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/chap3/pipeline-arch.png}
  \caption{Data pipeline architecture}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ingestion} converts raw sensor logs from a given field site into a unified operational table: acquisition streams recorded by heterogeneous systems at different sampling frequencies are joined and aligned, night-time observations are discarded, and the fault condition is assigned to a single shared label column. This conversion is the only stage that carries site-specific format knowledge; every downstream stage receives an identical schema and is fully insulated from the acquisition details of the source system.
    \item \textbf{Splitting} partitions the consolidated table into training, validation, and test sets according to a strict temporal grouping protocol. No sample-level transformation is performed at this stage; splitting is the gate at which evaluation integrity is established, and it must precede all subsequent stages that estimate statistics from the data.
    \item \textbf{Preprocessing} cleans the normal-class rows of the training partition through outlier removal (fault-labeled rows are exempt, as extreme values in those rows are expected fault signatures) and, for sites where the exploratory analysis identifies sustained temporal gaps in the field recording, tiered missing-value imputation, then estimates all cleaning statistics exclusively from that partition and applies them as fixed constants to validation and test.    
    \item \textbf{Feature engineering} transforms cleaned measurements into the representations consumed by the downstream models: named profiles specify which feature families are constructed, and a train-only selection stack eliminates redundant and low-relevance candidates before yielding the final feature matrices. The profile system makes this stage a controlled ablation instrument, isolating the contribution of each feature family across experiments.    
    \item \textbf{Model training} fits each candidate model on the feature-engineered partitions, restricts hyperparameter optimisation strictly to the training and validation partitions, and computes final metrics on the test partition exactly once after the winning configuration has been selected. All configurations, architectural choices, and evaluation results are tracked systematically, ensuring that every reported metric is traceable to a specific architectural state and data partition.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data Ingestion}
This step involves loading the raw data from the Costa dataset, then applying a series of transformations to output a unified table ready for the next stage.
These transformations include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Converting:} Convert original raw MAT files to Numpy arrays.
    \item \textbf{Filtering:} Remove negative irradiance values to resolve wrong sensor readings.
    \item \textbf{Daytime Trimming:} Filter out nighttime samples by setting a minimum irradiance threshold of 100 W/m².
    \item \textbf{Serializing:} Save the resulting table in a Parquet file and generates a JSON schema manifest.
\end{itemize}

\section{Splitting Protocol}
\label{sec:splitting}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Splitting is the stage at which the conditions for honest evaluation are either established or fatally compromised. The protocol adopted in this work rests on 3 principles, each enforced by a concrete implementation safeguard.

\textbf{Principle 1: Segment-based atomic units.} A \emph{segment} is a maximal contiguous run of measurements uninterrupted by either a temporal gap exceeding 300~seconds or a fault label transition. Segments are the atomic units of partitioning: no segment is ever split across train, validation, and test. This prevents row-level contamination from dense within-episode autocorrelation and ensures that each partition contains only complete, homogeneous operating episodes.

\textbf{Principle 2: Strict temporal ordering.} Partitions are assigned in chronological order: the training partition precedes validation, which precedes test. Because partition boundaries coincide with segment boundaries, adjacent partitions are separated by at least 300~seconds, which exceeds the longest rolling window used in feature engineering (30~seconds). No feature computed at evaluation time can therefore depend on samples from an earlier partition.

\textbf{Principle 3: Post-split windowing.} Sliding windows are constructed strictly within the boundaries of each partition, never across them. Window construction before splitting would allow overlapping windows to share samples from multiple partitions, creating a leakage channel that cannot be repaired retroactively. By deferring windowing to after the partition boundaries have been fixed, no 2 windows in different partitions ever share an underlying sample.

The partition ratios are 70 / 15 / 15 for training, validation, and test respectively. For anomaly detection, the training partition contains only normal-class atomic units, reflecting the semi-supervised assumption that labeled fault patterns are not available at training time; validation and test partitions contain the full label range and represent the realistic deployment scenario. For fault classification, atomic units are distributed across all 3 partitions with temporal-stratified assignment per fault class, so that each class has representation in all partitions while temporal ordering is preserved within each class.

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Preprocessing is conceived as a minimal cleaning layer that intervenes between split assignment and feature engineering. The guiding principle is that the model must learn to detect deviations from the expected operating behavior, and the preprocessing stage must preserve this signal while staying as lean as the underlying data quality allows.

For Costa, which contains 0 missing values after ingestion-time irradiance trimming, the active preprocessing layer consists solely of a scope-limited outlier removal step applied to the primary measured channels. The interquartile range of each primary electrical channel is computed exclusively on the normal-class rows of the training partition, and any normal-class training row whose value falls outside a bound of $3 \times \mathrm{IQR}$ is dropped. Fault-class rows are never subjected to outlier removal, because the extreme values present in those rows are precisely the signatures that the detection and classification systems are expected to learn. The multiplier of 3 (rather than the more aggressive value of 1.5) is chosen to target only values attributable to sensor malfunction or acquisition errors rather than to legitimate operational variability. Derived power channels are recomputed deterministically from the cleaned primary channels after outlier removal, which preserves the algebraic identity $P_{\mathrm{dc},s} = V_{\mathrm{dc},s} \cdot I_{\mathrm{dc},s}$ and prevents spurious mismatches between primary and derived quantities.

A general invariant is enforced across all preprocessing decisions. Any statistic estimated from the data, including outlier bounds and normalization factors, is computed exclusively from the training partition and applied as a constant to the validation and test partitions. This discipline preserves the independence of the evaluation surface and prevents any form of look-ahead leakage through preprocessing.

\section{Feature Engineering and Profile Ablation}
\label{sec:feature-engineering}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The feature engineering stage transforms cleaned operational measurements into the representations consumed by the downstream models. Two design principles govern its implementation. The first is that features are organized into named profiles, each corresponding to a coherent ablation level that strictly extends its predecessor, enabling a principled comparison of feature representations by contrasting adjacent profile levels. The second is that all selection statistics are estimated exclusively from the training partition, in keeping with the leakage-prevention discipline established in Section~\ref{sec:splitting}.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Features}

The starting point of the profile ladder is a compact set of physics-informed features motivated directly by the exploratory observations of Section~\ref{sec:eda}. These features encode domain knowledge about photovoltaic operation that a purely data-driven model would otherwise have to rediscover from raw observations, and they are constructed to remain numerically stable across the full operating range of the installation.

\paragraph{Family 1: Irradiance-normalized channels.}
Under healthy operation, the per-string power and current scale approximately linearly with irradiance, making $P_{\mathrm{dc},s}/G$ and $I_{\mathrm{dc},s}/G$ approximately constant. Dividing by irradiance collapses the large diurnal amplitude swing and exposes the fault-related residual. The denominator is floored at $G_{\min} = 1$~W/m$^2$ to prevent instability at dawn and dusk:
\[
    \tilde{P}_{\mathrm{dc},s} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{dc},s}}{\max(G,\, G_{\min})}, \qquad
    \tilde{I}_{\mathrm{dc},s} = \frac{I_{\mathrm{dc},s}}{\max(G,\, G_{\min})}.
\]

\paragraph{Family 2: Per-string imbalance descriptors.}
For a two-string installation where both strings are individually instrumented, the symmetric normalized difference between strings isolates localized faults that do not manifest in plant-aggregated quantities. Three imbalance features are computed:
\[
    \Delta P = \frac{P_{\mathrm{dc},1} - P_{\mathrm{dc},2}}{P_{\mathrm{dc},1} + P_{\mathrm{dc},2} + \varepsilon}, \quad
    \Delta I = \frac{I_{\mathrm{dc},1} - I_{\mathrm{dc},2}}{I_{\mathrm{dc},1} + I_{\mathrm{dc},2} + \varepsilon}, \quad
    \Delta V = \frac{V_{\mathrm{dc},1} - V_{\mathrm{dc},2}}{V_{\mathrm{dc},1} + V_{\mathrm{dc},2} + \varepsilon},
\]
where $\varepsilon$ is a small stability constant. Under healthy, symmetric operation all three ratios are near zero; a localized fault on one string shifts the affected ratio away from zero.

\paragraph{Family 3: Temperature-corrected power.}
The maximum power point of crystalline silicon modules decreases linearly with temperature above the standard test condition reference $T_{\mathrm{ref}} = 25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, at a rate given by the temperature coefficient $\gamma_{P_{\max}}$. The temperature-corrected, irradiance-normalized power isolates the fault-related deviation from the thermal component:
\[
    \tilde{P}_{\mathrm{corr}} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{dc}}}{\bigl(1 + \gamma_{P_{\max}}(T - T_{\mathrm{ref}})\bigr)\cdot\max(G,\, G_{\min})}.
\]

\paragraph{Family 4: First-order temporal derivatives.}
Discrete first differences of the primary channels expose rapid transients characteristic of abrupt fault types such as short circuits and open circuits:
\[
    \delta P_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t) = P_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t) - P_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t-1), \quad
    \delta V_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t) = V_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t) - V_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t-1), \quad
    \delta I_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t) = I_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t) - I_{\mathrm{dc},s}(t-1).
\]

Together, the 4 families yield approximately 17 engineered features after the selection stack described below has pruned the candidate pool. This feature set defines the profile \texttt{plus\_physics}, which serves as the principal representation for the headline experiments in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}.

\subsection{Statistical Features}

The second feature family extends \texttt{plus\_physics} with causal segment-safe rolling statistics that provide the model with a compact summary of recent signal history. All windows are strictly causal: at time $t$, only samples $\{t-w+1, \ldots, t\}$ are used, and rolling computations never cross episode segment boundaries.

For each physics-informed feature $x$ and window size $w \in \{10, 30\}$ (seconds, given 1~Hz sampling), two aggregate statistics are computed:

\paragraph{Rolling mean.}
\[
    \bar{x}_{w}(t) = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{k=0}^{w-1} x(t - k).
\]

\paragraph{Rolling standard deviation.}
\[
    \sigma_{x,w}(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{w} \sum_{k=0}^{w-1} \bigl(x(t-k) - \bar{x}_{w}(t)\bigr)^{2}}.
\]

These statistics are computed independently per episode segment so that no window ever spans an episode boundary. The rolling standard deviation is particularly informative for fault detection: fault-onset events produce sharp transients that increase local variance, while the rolling mean tracks gradual drift in the irradiance-normalized channels.

The 3 feature profiles evaluated in this thesis are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{baseline\_raw}: the cleaned sensor columns produced by preprocessing, with no engineered features. This profile provides the reference level against which the contribution of each feature family is measured.
    \item \texttt{plus\_physics}: extends \texttt{baseline\_raw} by adding the 4 physics-informed feature families of Section~\ref{sec:feature-engineering}. This is the primary profile used for the headline results in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}.
    \item \texttt{plus\_rolling}: extends \texttt{plus\_physics} by adding the rolling mean and rolling standard deviation over windows $w \in \{10, 30\}$ for each physics-informed feature.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Selection Stack}

The candidate feature pool is pruned by a 2-stage train-only selector stack before the final feature set is written to disk. The first stage is hygiene pruning: near-constant columns whose variance falls below a numerical threshold are removed, exact duplicate columns are collapsed, and deterministic algebraic aliases (such as the identity $P_{\mathrm{dc}} = P_{\mathrm{dc},1} + P_{\mathrm{dc},2}$ identified during exploratory analysis) are detected and reduced to a single representative. This stage requires no label information and therefore introduces no leakage risk.

The second stage applies minimum-redundancy maximum-relevance (mRMR) feature selection under the mutual information difference (MID) criterion \citep{peng2005mrmr}, with a target retained pool of 32 features after hygiene. The relevance term scores each candidate by its mutual information with the task label; the redundancy term penalizes mutual information against already-retained features; the greedy selection proceeds until the target size is reached or the pool is exhausted. All mutual information estimates are computed from the training partition only. For large training partitions, estimation is performed on a stratified, group-aware subset of up to 30\,000 rows, which preserves the statistical structure of the label distribution and the temporal grouping while keeping the selection step tractable within the project's computational budget.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:evaluation-methodology}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This section defines the evaluation protocols applied uniformly across all experiments. It specifies the primary metrics for each task, the leakage validation checks, the cross-validation scheme, and the threshold calibration procedure. The choice of evaluation metrics constitutes the third tier of the class imbalance handling strategy, complementing the paradigm-level and training-level responses described in earlier sections: metrics are selected to reflect per-class diagnostic capability rather than aggregate accuracy, preventing majority-class prevalence from inflating the reported figures.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

The performance figures reported throughout this work are expressed in terms of four confusion-matrix quantities: true positives ($\mathrm{TP}$), true negatives ($\mathrm{TN}$), false positives ($\mathrm{FP}$), and false negatives ($\mathrm{FN}$).

\paragraph{Precision.}
Precision measures the fraction of all positive predictions that are correct, or equivalently the proportion of raised alarms that correspond to actual fault events.
\[
  \mathrm{Precision} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}.
\]

\paragraph{Recall.}
Recall (sensitivity) measures the fraction of all actual positives that are correctly identified, or equivalently the proportion of real fault events that are detected by the system.
\[
  \mathrm{Recall} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}.
\]

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
Accuracy measures the fraction of all predictions, whether positive or negative, that are correct.
\[
  \mathrm{Accuracy} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}}.
\]
On class-imbalanced data, accuracy is dominated by the majority class and provides little diagnostic information about minority-class performance.

\paragraph{F1 Score.}
The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. It provides a single balanced measure that accounts for both missed faults (low recall) and unreliable alarms (low precision).
\[
  \mathrm{F1} = \frac{2\cdot\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\]

\paragraph{Macro-Averaged Score.}
When a metric is computed per class in a multi-class setting, the macro average takes the unweighted mean over all $K$ classes:
\[
  \mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{macro}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{Score}_k.
\]
Macro averaging assigns equal importance to every class regardless of sample count, so a failure on a rare fault class is penalized as strongly as a failure on the majority class.

\paragraph{Weighted-Averaged Score.}
The weighted average weights each per-class score by the number of samples $n_k$ of that class:
\[
  \mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{weighted}} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k\cdot\mathrm{Score}_k}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k}.
\]
Weighted averaging reflects the actual class distribution. On imbalanced data it is dominated by majority classes and can conceal failure on rare classes.

\paragraph{Precision-Recall AUC.}
As the decision threshold is swept continuously, each threshold yields a (Precision, Recall) pair; the full set of pairs traces the precision-recall curve. The area under this curve (PR-AUC) is:
\[
  \mathrm{PR\text{-}AUC} = \int_0^1 P(R)\,\mathrm{d}R,
\]
where $P(R)$ denotes precision as a function of recall \citep{saito2015pr}. A random detector achieves a PR-AUC equal to the positive-class prevalence; a perfect detector achieves~1.0. Because PR-AUC integrates over all possible thresholds, it is a threshold-independent measure of intrinsic ranking quality: it reflects how well a model separates positives from negatives regardless of where the operating point is ultimately placed.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol per Task}

\paragraph{Task~A: Anomaly Detection.}

Since PR-AUC is threshold-independent and measures intrinsic ranking quality, it is the primary criterion for model comparison in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}. The headline figure is the \textbf{macro per-class PR-AUC}, obtained by computing the binary PR-AUC of each fault class versus normal independently and averaging the results without class weighting. The \textbf{worst-class PR-AUC} is reported alongside it to ensure that no individual class is overlooked by the aggregate figure. Given the edge deployment context, \textbf{model size} is reported as a secondary comparison criterion alongside PR-AUC: two models with comparable ranking quality are differentiated by the number of trainable parameters, as the target platform imposes strict memory and computational constraints detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:deployment}.

\textbf{Evaluation granularity.} Task~A models assign anomaly scores at different natural units depending on their architecture; PR-AUC is computed at the granularity native to each model:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sample level:} one score per measurement vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$, for models that process individual time steps.
  \item \textbf{Window level:} one score per sliding context window $\mathbf{X}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{w \times d}$, for models that require a fixed-length input sequence.
  \item \textbf{Episode level:} scores are aggregated over a contiguous fault episode, defined as a temporally uninterrupted sequence of fault-labeled measurements bounded by a label transition. This granularity reflects whether a fault event as a whole was detected.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Operating-point characterization.} Once the winning model has been identified through PR-AUC, secondary operating-point figures (precision, recall, and F1 at a calibrated threshold) are reported. The threshold calibration procedure is described in Section~\ref{sec:threshold-calibration}.

\paragraph{Task~B: Fault Classification.}

The criterion used to compare classifiers is the \textbf{macro-averaged F1 score}. Accuracy is dominated by the normal class and is therefore uninformative under class imbalance. Weighted F1 is dominated by the shadowing class (label~4, 35.7\% of fault-labeled rows) and can conceal failure on the three rare electrically induced fault classes. Macro F1 assigns equal weight to every fault type and penalizes failure on any class regardless of its frequency. Beyond the headline figure, the full per-class precision, recall, and F1 triplet is reported for each fault class individually, alongside weighted F1 and overall accuracy as secondary aggregate references.

\subsection{Leakage Validation Suite}

Leakage checks fall into two categories: checks that are \emph{guaranteed by pipeline design} and only verified programmatically, and checks that require \emph{post-training inspection} of model behavior.

\paragraph{By-design checks.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Time-split integrity.} The maximum training timestamp must strictly precede the minimum validation timestamp, which must precede the minimum test timestamp. This property is guaranteed by the segment-based temporal ordering of Section~\ref{sec:splitting}; the check confirms no implementation error has violated it.
    \item \textbf{Duplicate sample check.} No feature row in the validation or test set may be an exact duplicate of a training row. This property is guaranteed by the post-split windowing principle of Section~\ref{sec:splitting}; the check confirms it holds after feature construction.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Post-training checks.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Label shuffle test.} The check is implemented differently for the two tasks because their training paradigms differ structurally. For fault classification, the model is retrained on randomly permuted training labels; validation performance must collapse to the chance level; retained performance is a symptom of target leakage through features that encode label information by construction. For anomaly detection, the model is never retrained: it was trained without any labels. Instead, the anomaly scores are held fixed and the evaluation labels (validation and test) are permuted; the resulting PR-AUC must collapse to the positive-class base rate. If shuffled PR-AUC remains materially above the base rate, the metric computation is artifactual rather than the model being discriminative.
    \item \textbf{Feature importance audit.} A single-feature classifier is trained on each feature independently; any feature whose standalone score approaches the full-model score is flagged as a potential proxy label, indicating that it may have been inadvertently derived from or correlated with the target.
    \item \textbf{Performance sanity check.} Reported metrics are compared against the floor (random baseline at class prevalence) and an approximate ceiling derived from the dataset's class separability. Values that exceed any published baseline by an unexplained margin are flagged for investigation.
\end{itemize}

A training run that fails any post-training check does not yield a reportable result; the failure is logged and its cause investigated before the run is retried.

\subsection{Cross-Validation Protocol}

All experiments use a \textbf{5-fold episode-stratified cross-validation} scheme. Folds are constructed at the episode level: each fold corresponds to a distinct partition of the episode set, so no episode appears in both the validation and test splits of any fold, and temporal ordering is preserved within folds.

The two tasks impose structurally different fold constructions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anomaly detection.} The training partition of each fold contains exclusively normal-class episodes; fault episodes are distributed only across the validation and test splits. This preserves the one-class learning assumption across all folds: the detector is never exposed to labeled fault data during training, and the full fault episode set rotates through evaluation across folds.
    \item \textbf{Fault classification.} All labeled classes are present in the training partition of each fold, with temporal-stratified assignment ensuring that each fault class has evaluation coverage in every fold while respecting chronological ordering within each class.
\end{itemize}




\section{Threshold Calibration Policy}
\label{sec:threshold-calibration}

The primary evaluation metric for anomaly detection, PR-AUC, is a \emph{threshold-free} measure: it summarizes the quality of the score ranking across the entire precision-recall trade-off curve and is insensitive to the choice of any single operating threshold. A model with high PR-AUC produces a ranking in which fault samples tend to receive higher anomaly scores than normal samples, regardless of where the decision boundary is placed. However, deployment requires choosing one operating point on this curve: the threshold at which the continuous score is converted into a binary alarm. The quality of this operating point determines the actual precision and recall of the deployed system, and a poorly calibrated threshold can substantially degrade operational performance even when ranking quality is high.

The calibration challenge is that the threshold must be set entirely from training-normal scores, without access to fault-labeled data, to remain consistent with the semi-supervised formulation. A naive approach based on an empirical score percentile implicitly assumes a parametric tail shape that may not hold in practice. To address this, the calibration procedure applies Extreme Value Theory: the upper tail of the normal-class score distribution is modeled by fitting a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) to the exceedances above a high quantile of training-normal scores, using the Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT) method \citep{pickands1975statistical, coles2001extreme}. The GPD fit extrapolates the tail rather than reading off an empirical count, and the calibrated threshold is derived as the GPD quantile corresponding to a target false-positive rate. This procedure is applied uniformly across all detectors, so cross-model comparisons reflect architectural differences in ranking quality rather than differences in threshold policy.

A single operating point, however, collapses a rich anomaly score into one binary decision and imposes the same precision-recall compromise on every fault, irrespective of its severity. The deployed system therefore exposes a three-tier escalation policy modelled on the ISA-18.2 alarm-management standard \citep{isa182011alarm}, which prescribes that alarms be prioritized by severity so that an operator triages a structured hierarchy rather than reacting to an undifferentiated stream of identical alerts. Each tier is a distinct operating point on the \emph{same} anomaly score, fixed by a different calibration rule and serving a different purpose; together they form a sequential escalation chain (P3~$\rightarrow$~P2~$\rightarrow$~P1) of increasing confidence and severity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{P3 (Advisory).} The GPD/POT threshold described above, set to a sensitive false-positive rate ($\text{FPR} \leq 20\%$). It favours recall as a first-line alert that surfaces candidate anomalies early.
    \item \textbf{P2 (High).} A conformal-prediction threshold \citep{vovk2005algorithmic} at significance $\alpha = 0.05$, which provides a \emph{distribution-free} guarantee that the false-positive rate does not exceed 5\%. It trades recall for precision relative to P3.
    \item \textbf{P1 (Critical).} A one-sided CUSUM rule \citep{page1954cusum} that accumulates evidence across consecutive samples and fires only on \emph{persistent} deviations, suppressing the transient spikes that trip the lower tiers. Being sequential, it is characterized by detection delay and mean time between false alarms ($\text{ARL}_0$) rather than by per-sample precision and recall.
\end{itemize}

All three thresholds are calibrated entirely from training-normal scores, without access to fault labels, so the policy remains consistent with the semi-supervised formulation. The tiers are characterized on held-out fault episodes in Section~\ref{sec:ad-deployment}, and surfaced to the operator through the monitoring interface in Chapter~\ref{ch:deployment}.


\section{Probability Calibration}

Gradient boosting classifiers produce a vector of class scores that sum to one and are conventionally interpreted as class probabilities. However, these scores are not guaranteed to be calibrated: a model may assign a confidence of 0.9 to a fault class while being correct only 60\% of the time at that confidence level. This miscalibration is invisible to discrimination metrics such as macro F1, which measure ranking quality rather than probabilistic accuracy. In the context of this work it matters because the monitoring interface presents the fault class confidence alongside the diagnostic label; miscalibrated scores degrade the operator's ability to triage alerts proportionally to their actual reliability.

Post-hoc probability calibration applies to fault classification only. In anomaly detection, the semi-supervised formulation precludes it: fitting a calibrator requires binary fault/normal labels at training time, which are unavailable by design, and the anomaly score is an unbounded continuous quantity rather than a class probability. Anomaly detection instead derives probabilistic operating points through conformal p-values, which provide finite-sample false-alarm guarantees without requiring fault-labeled calibration data. For fault classification, labeled data are available across all classes and gradient boosting models already output probability-like class scores that can be meaningfully recalibrated.

Platt scaling \citep{platt1999probabilistic} fits a sigmoid function to the model's raw output scores using the validation set, mapping them to better-calibrated probability estimates without modifying the underlying model or its discrimination performance. Fitting on the validation set, rather than the training set, prevents the calibrator from exploiting the same distribution the base model was optimized on.

Calibration quality is assessed through three complementary metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brier score.} The mean squared error between the predicted class probability vector $\hat{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^K$ and the one-hot ground truth $y_i \in \{0,1\}^K$, averaged over all samples and classes:
    \[
        \mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} (\hat{p}_{ik} - y_{ik})^2.
    \]
    A perfect calibrator achieves $\mathrm{BS} = 0$; a model that always predicts the class prior achieves $\mathrm{BS} = \bar{p}(1-\bar{p})$.

    \item \textbf{Expected Calibration Error (ECE).} Predictions are partitioned into $M$ equal-width confidence bins. Within each bin $B_m$, the gap between mean predicted confidence and observed accuracy is computed; the ECE averages these gaps weighted by bin population:
    \[
        \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{N} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|,
    \]
    where $\mathrm{acc}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i \in B_m}\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_i = y_i]$ and $\mathrm{conf}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i \in B_m}\max_k \hat{p}_{ik}$.

    \item \textbf{Maximum Calibration Error (MCE).} The worst-case bin-level miscalibration:
    \[
        \mathrm{MCE} = \max_{m \in \{1,\ldots,M\}} \left| \mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m) \right|.
    \]
    MCE is sensitive to the most poorly calibrated confidence region, complementing the average-case view of ECE.
\end{itemize}

Together, these metrics supplement the discrimination-oriented headline figures and establish the reliability of the probability outputs presented to the operator in the monitoring interface.


The 5-fold scheme serves as the variance estimator for reported metrics: mean $\pm$ standard deviation across folds replaces bootstrap confidence intervals, providing an estimate of metric variability under different episode partitions. Model selection uses the fold-average validation score; the test partition of each fold is evaluated exactly once after the winning configuration has been selected, and test scores are never used to guide selection.
