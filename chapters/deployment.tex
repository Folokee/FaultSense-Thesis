\section{ONNX Export and Artifact Contract}
\label{sec:deployment-onnx}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The trained PC-Flow model is exported to the Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) format using \texttt{torch.onnx.export}. The export procedure is designed to produce a batch-generalizable ONNX graph rather than a batch-1-baked artifact: coupling layer masking uses \texttt{index\_select} (which maps to ONNX \texttt{Gather}) and concatenation rather than boolean-mask indexing, which would bake the batch dimension as a constant. The ONNX graph is verified using \texttt{onnxruntime} on a held-out calibration set to confirm numerical agreement with the PyTorch reference to a tolerance of $10^{-5}$ on the log-likelihood output.

Each deployed artifact is accompanied by a \textbf{deployment contract}: a structured metadata file that records:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the expected input tensor shape (batch, feature dimension);
    \item the ordered list of feature names, in the order expected by the model;
    \item the StandardScaler parameters (mean and scale) estimated from the training partition;
    \item the physics conditioning channels $c = (\text{irr}, \text{pvt})$ and their indices in the feature vector;
    \item the calibrated tier parameters consumed by the alarm engine (Section~\ref{sec:threshold-calibration}): the sensitive (P3) threshold, the conformal (P2) threshold, the CUSUM (P1) reference slack $k$ and control limit $h$, and the normal-score statistics $(\mu_{\mathrm{normal}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{normal}})$ used for the CUSUM $z$-transform;
    \item the timestamp of the training run and the validation score at checkpoint.
\end{itemize}
The contract is verified at load time before inference begins: the runtime checks that the feature list in the incoming data stream matches the contract's ordered feature list, applies the stored scaler, and validates the threshold provenance. Any contract violation raises a runtime exception before the first inference is executed.

\section{Drift Detection}
\label{sec:deployment-drift}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A deployed anomaly detector is only reliable as long as the operational data distribution remains close to the training distribution. PV system behaviour evolves over time: module ageing, seasonal soiling, inverter firmware changes, and weather regime shifts can all move the distribution of normal-class NLL scores, causing the calibrated threshold to become either too permissive or too restrictive without any indication to the operator.

The drift detection module monitors the physics channel $\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{corr}}$ (temperature-corrected, irradiance-normalised power) using a bidirectional \textbf{Page-Hinkley (PH) test} \citep{page1954continuous}. The physics channel is used as the primary trigger rather than the model score itself to avoid the circularity that arises when a stale model's own score is used to judge whether the model is stale. The score channel is monitored in parallel as an advisory-only readout. The PH statistic accumulates signed deviations from a running reference:

\[
    M_t = \sum_{i=1}^{t}\bigl(x_i - \bar{x} - \delta\bigr), \quad
    \mathrm{PH}_t = M_t - \min_{1 \leq j \leq t} M_j, \quad
    \text{drift} = \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{PH}_t > \lambda],
\]

where $\bar{x}$ is the running mean, $\delta$ is a sensitivity margin against ordinary fluctuations, and $\lambda$ is the control limit. The monitor escalates through two stages — \emph{advisory} after $N_{\mathrm{adv}}$ consecutive PH detections, and \emph{action-ready} after $N_{\mathrm{act}} > N_{\mathrm{adv}}$ — with a 1-hour cooldown that suppresses repeated events after an operator action. Figure~\ref{fig:deployment-drift-tikz} illustrates the PH accumulator behaviour over a simulated deployment window.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering

\section{Real-Time Inference Performance}
\label{sec:deployment-latency}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A central premise of this work is that PV fault detection and diagnosis can be performed \emph{on the edge} — locally, on hardware inexpensive enough to be installed per site — rather than streaming raw measurements to a cloud service for every inference. Edge execution removes the round-trip latency, the dependence on continuous connectivity, and the recurring transmission cost that a cloud-inference architecture would impose on a permanently running monitor. This section validates that premise empirically: it shows that the full two-model pipeline meets the real-time constraint on the target device with a wide margin, and that the margin is a structural property of the design rather than an artifact of one configuration.

\paragraph{Measurement protocol.} The complete pipeline — feature engineering, PC-Flow anomaly scoring, the tiered alarm engine, and conditional fault classification — was exercised on the Jetson Nano by replaying 162\,453 real PV samples in acquisition order at the operational cadence of 1~Hz. Wall-clock time was recorded around each per-sample inference call, and the first 50 samples were discarded as warm-up. Latency is reported per stage and end-to-end as the median (p50), 95th, and 99th percentiles over the full replay (Table~\ref{tab:deployment-latency}). At 1~Hz the per-sample budget is 1\,000~ms.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Inference latency on the Jetson Nano (162\,453 samples at 1~Hz; budget 1\,000~ms).}
\label{tab:deployment-latency}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{p50 (ms)} & \textbf{p95 (ms)} & \textbf{p99 (ms)} \\
\midrule
PC-Flow anomaly detector  & 8.97  & 13.58 & 19.04 \\
Extra Trees classifier    & 5.10  & 9.50  & 14.75 \\
\midrule
Full pipeline             & \textbf{12.94} & \textbf{21.44} & \textbf{30.04} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The full pipeline completes in 12.94~ms at the median and 30.04~ms at the 99th percentile, a margin of $77\times$ and $33\times$ respectively against the 1\,000~ms budget. Because the classifier is invoked only when the detector raises an alarm, the steady-state cost during healthy operation — the overwhelming majority of samples at a sound installation — is the detector path alone, at 8.97~ms median.

\paragraph{Inference cost breakdown.} When the PC-Flow model is timed in isolation — excluding feature engineering, alarm-engine routing, and dashboard broadcast — it completes in \textbf{565~µs} at the median and \textbf{970~µs} at the 99th percentile, accounting for less than 5\% of the total end-to-end latency. The dominant cost is therefore the surrounding pipeline, not the model itself.

This observation is consequential for the design rationale. PC-Flow was selected over larger deep baselines not only because it matched their detection quality (Section~\ref{sec:ad-tournament}), but because a compact detector is a prerequisite for a complete monitoring system to run within a single 1~Hz inference cycle on one device: the fault classifier, the alarm engine, and the drift monitor all execute in the same loop alongside the detector. A substantially larger model would remain within the 1\,000~ms per-sample budget in isolation, but would consume the latency margin required by the co-running components.


In summary, the pipeline meets the real-time requirement with a $33\times$ margin at the 99th percentile, and does so in a regime where orchestration, not inference, is the dominant cost. The system satisfies the edge-deployment objective in full, and retains clear headroom for higher sampling rates, additional monitored channels, or richer feature pipelines without approaching the real-time limit.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:deployment-conclusion}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This chapter has presented the complete deployment of the FaultSense monitoring system on the NVIDIA Jetson Nano. The artifact contract (Section~\ref{sec:deployment-onnx}) enforces consistency between the training environment and the operational inference environment at load time. The ISA-18.2 three-tier alarm system (P3~Advisory, P2~High, P1~Critical) maps three calibrated threshold policies — sensitive, conformal, and CUSUM — to actionable priority levels that allow plant operators to triage alerts without reading alert text. The software stack (Python backend, vanilla-JS frontend, WebSocket communication) was chosen to minimise dependencies on the constrained aarch64 platform while delivering a responsive single-page operator interface. The Page-Hinkley drift detector monitors the physics channel independently of the model score to avoid circularity, and escalates through advisory and action-ready stages before enabling the operator-gated model update workflow.
