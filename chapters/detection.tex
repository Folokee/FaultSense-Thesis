\chapter{Anomaly Detection}\label{ch:anomaly-detection}

\section{Introduction}
Anomaly detection is the process of distinguishing between normal and anomalous states in a PV system. This chapter introduces the problem, 
then presents the explored models, and finally discusses the experimental results.

\section{Task Formulation and Methodology}
Semi-supervised learning is a machine learning technique that trains models using a small amount of labeled data and a large amount of unlabeled data.
The anomaly detection task can be seen as a semi-supervised learning problem, this approach is ideal for our case because it allows us to leverage the limited labeled data available.
The various methods discussed below follow a common framework:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Train a model on normal-behavior data to extract generalizable signatures of normal operation.
    \item Derive a scoring function that measures deviation from normality—higher scores correspond to a higher likelihood of an anomaly.
    \item Calibrate a threshold from this scoring function. During inference, the model computes the score and flags the current state as anomalous if the score exceeds the threshold.
\end{itemize}

\section{Model Implementation and Hyperparameter Optimization}

This section describes the implementation of the anomaly detection model lineup and the hyperparameter optimization (HPO) protocol used to select the final configurations. The implementation follows a shared experimental contract across models: a unified split interface, a consistent scoring convention (higher scores indicate higher anomaly likelihood), a threshold calibration stage executed on validation scores, and a common reporting suite that includes prevalence-aware metrics.

\subsection{Shared Experimental Infrastructure}\label{sec:ad-shared-infra}

\paragraph{Training paradigm and splits.}
Unless stated otherwise, detectors are trained in a semi-supervised manner on normal-only data and evaluated on both normal and fault samples. The training, validation, and test partitions are generated by the splitting protocol presented in Section~\ref{sec:implementation-splitting}. For semi-supervised anomaly detection, the training partition contains only normal segments, while all fault segments are held out to validation and test. This discipline ensures that the anomaly score reflects deviations from the nominal operating manifold rather than direct exposure to fault labels.

\paragraph{Deep learning windowing.}
The deep learning (DL) detectors operate on fixed-length time windows. A shared \texttt{TimeSeriesDataset} abstraction slices the post-split dataframe into windows of size $W$ with stride $s$, returning tensors of shape $[B, W, F]$, where $B$ is the batch size and $F$ the number of input features. Window generation respects segment boundaries: windows never cross continuity gaps or label-transition boundaries, preventing implicit leakage through concatenated sequences. The dataset interface optionally returns the original per-sample labels and a group identifier used to aggregate or reset stateful scoring at segment boundaries.

\paragraph{Score-to-alarm threshold calibration.}
Most detectors emit continuous anomaly scores, which must be converted into binary alarms for reporting and deployment. Threshold selection is performed exclusively on the validation partition. Two calibration strategies are supported. The default strategy models the upper tail of the normal-class score distribution using a Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT) approach with a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) fit. Given a POT quantile $q$ (default $q=0.90$), exceedances over the $q$-quantile of normal validation scores are fitted, and a threshold is selected to target a specified tail probability (default 0.05), corresponding to an expected false-alarm rate on normal data. When a tail fit is numerically unstable (e.g., too few exceedances), a quantile fallback is used and the threshold is set to a fixed percentile (default 95\%) of normal validation scores.

\subsubsection{Bayesian Online Change Point Detection (BOCD)}

The Bayesian Online Change Point Detection (BOCD) detector models each feature independently using a Normal-Inverse-Gamma (NIG) conjugate prior, and forms a joint multivariate predictive density as the product of univariate Student-$t$ predictives. The sequential run-length posterior is then used to compute a predictive mixture likelihood, which decreases sharply when the observed sequence becomes unlikely under the current run.

\paragraph{} As in the other classical baselines, inputs are standardized using a \texttt{StandardScaler} fitted on the training partition. During \texttt{fit}, the NIG prior parameters are initialized from the training statistics, with $\mu_0$ set to the per-feature training mean and $\beta_0$ set to $\alpha_0$ times the per-feature training variance.

\paragraph{} BOCD maintains a run-length distribution with a geometric hazard prior controlled by \texttt{hazard\_lambda} (denoted $\lambda$), where the changepoint probability is $h=1/\lambda$. The run-length posterior is truncated to $R_{\mathrm{trunc}}=200$ to keep memory and compute bounded by $\mathcal{O}(R_{\mathrm{trunc}}\times F)$.

\paragraph{} The anomaly score is defined as the negative log predictive likelihood under the run-length mixture. Let $R_t(r)$ be the run-length posterior at time $t$ and $p(x_t\mid r)$ the corresponding predictive density; the score is computed as:
\begin{equation}
    s_t = -\log\left(\sum_{r=0}^{R_{\mathrm{trunc}}} R_t(r)\, p(x_t\mid r)\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{} To avoid state carryover across operational gaps, scoring is group-aware: the run-length distribution is reset at each segment boundary (\texttt{episode\_id} for Path~A and \texttt{operating\_day\_id} for Path~B). The first score in every segment is therefore computed from the global prior fitted on the training partition.

\paragraph{} When hyperparameter optimization is enabled, Optuna is used with the TPE sampler for 35 trials, tuning only \texttt{hazard\_lambda}.

\subsection{Novel Approaches}
In this section, we present novel model architectures developed for this exact task, these approaches are based on deep learning and explore complementary features from classical appraches, as well as from the literature. 
\subsubsection{Mamba Adaptive Anomaly Transformer (MAAT)}
The proposed Mamba Adaptive Anomaly Transformer (MAAT) is a deep semi-supervised anomaly detector designed for multivariate PV time-series data. The architecture combines sparse anomaly attention with a parallel Mamba state-space branch and a minimax association learning objective. Unlike classical reconstruction-based autoencoders, MAAT jointly models reconstruction fidelity and temporal association consistency, allowing the detector to identify subtle deviations in temporal dynamics even when reconstruction error alone remains low.

\paragraph{} The overall architecture is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:maat_architecture}. The model receives an input window
\[
x \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times W \times F},
\]
where $B$ denotes the batch size, $W$ the temporal window length, and $F$ the number of features. The input is first transformed through a \texttt{DataEmbedding} module composed of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a linear token embedding projecting $F \rightarrow d_{\text{model}}$,
    \item a sinusoidal positional embedding,
    \item and a dropout layer applied after summation.
\end{itemize}

The resulting representation is:
\[
H^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times W \times d_{\text{model}}}.
\]

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/detection/maat-architecture.png}
    \caption{Overall architecture of the proposed MAAT model combining sparse anomaly attention and Mamba state-space modeling.}
    \label{fig:maat_architecture}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{} The encoder consists of $L$ stacked \texttt{EncoderLayer} blocks. Each layer contains two parallel temporal modeling branches: a sparse anomaly-attention branch and a Mamba state-space branch. Their outputs are fused through a learned gating mechanism before being processed by a position-wise feed-forward network.

\paragraph{} The anomaly-attention branch computes two distinct temporal association matrices:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \emph{series association} $S$, learned through block-local sparse self-attention,
    \item and the \emph{prior association} $P$, defined by a Gaussian kernel parameterized by learned variance terms.
\end{itemize}

Given queries $Q$, keys $K$, and values $V$, the series association is computed as:

\begin{equation}
S = \mathrm{Softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right),
\end{equation}

where attention is restricted to positions within the same temporal block of size \texttt{block\_size}. This sparse attention mechanism constrains the model to learn local temporal dependencies characteristic of normal PV operation.

\paragraph{} In parallel, the prior association is modeled using a Gaussian kernel:

\begin{equation}
P_{i,j} \propto
\exp\left(
-\frac{|i-j|^2}{2\sigma_i^2}
\right),
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_i$ is learned dynamically through a dedicated linear projection layer. The prior distribution represents an inductive bias describing expected local temporal continuity under normal operating conditions.

\paragraph{} Unlike standard attention mechanisms, only the learned series association $S$ is used for value aggregation:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = S \cdot V.
\end{equation}

The Gaussian prior $P$ is used exclusively for discrepancy-based anomaly scoring and minimax optimization.

\paragraph{} Parallel to the attention branch, each encoder layer contains a Mamba selective state-space model (SSM) branch. The Mamba block models long-range temporal dependencies with linear computational complexity, enabling efficient processing of long PV sequences while preserving sequential dynamics that may not be captured by local attention alone.

\paragraph{} The outputs of the attention and Mamba branches are fused through a learned gating mechanism:

\begin{equation}
G = \sigma\left(
W_g [H_{\mathrm{mamba}} \, \| \, H_{\mathrm{attn}}]
\right),
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{fused}}
=
G \odot H_{\mathrm{mamba}}
+
(1-G) \odot H_{\mathrm{attn}},
\end{equation}

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the sigmoid activation and $\odot$ the element-wise product. This mechanism allows the network to adaptively determine whether local attention-based patterns or long-range state-space dynamics should dominate for a given temporal context.

\paragraph{} The fused representation is processed through residual connections, Layer Normalization, and a position-wise feed-forward network implemented using $1\times1$ convolutions with GELU activation. After the final encoder layer, a linear projection reconstructs the original feature space:

\[
\hat{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times W \times F}.
\]

\paragraph{} MAAT is trained using a minimax objective combining reconstruction error and association discrepancy. Let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}$ denote the reconstruction mean squared error and $\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)$ the Kullback--Leibler divergence. The training objective is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
+
k
\left[
\mathrm{KL}(P \| S)
-
\mathrm{KL}(S \| P)
\right],
\end{equation}

where $k$ controls the balance between reconstruction and association learning. This minimax formulation encourages the learned series associations to diverge maximally from the Gaussian prior on normal data, thereby amplifying deviations during anomalous conditions.

\paragraph{} During inference, anomaly scores are computed using both reconstruction error and association discrepancy. The discrepancy between the series and prior associations is first estimated as:

\begin{equation}
D_t
=
\mathrm{KL}(S \| P)
+
\mathrm{KL}(P \| S).
\end{equation}

The discrepancy is transformed into temporal importance weights using a temperature-scaled softmax:

\begin{equation}
w_t
=
\mathrm{Softmax}(-T D_t),
\end{equation}

where $T$ is the temperature hyperparameter. The final timestep-level anomaly score is then computed as:

\begin{equation}
A_t
=
w_t \cdot \|x_t - \hat{x}_t\|^2.
\end{equation}

Window-level anomaly scores are obtained by taking the maximum score over the temporal dimension. This formulation ensures that anomalies are detected only when both temporal associations are disrupted and reconstruction quality deteriorates.

\paragraph{} MAAT is trained exclusively on healthy operating data, following a semi-supervised anomaly detection paradigm. Hyperparameter optimization is performed using Optuna with a two-stage Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) search strategy. The first stage optimizes training-related parameters such as learning rate, dropout, temperature, and reconstruction weight, while the second stage optimizes architectural parameters including window size, block size, embedding dimension, number of heads, encoder depth, and feed-forward dimension.

\paragraph{} To ensure consistent thresholding across all evaluated baselines, the anomaly threshold is calibrated using a Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT) Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) fitted on validation-normal scores.
