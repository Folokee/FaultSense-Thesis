\section*{Context}

The global energy landscape is undergoing a profound transformation, marked by a gradual reduction in fossil-fuel dependence and the growing integration of sustainable energy sources into modern energy systems. Among the available renewable technologies, solar photovoltaic (PV) energy has emerged as one of the most dynamic and widely adopted solutions. Its growth has been remarkable: by the end of 2025, global cumulative installed PV capacity reached approximately 2,383~GW \citep{irena2026}. This expansion has been supported by continuous reductions in module costs, improvements in conversion efficiency, large-scale industrial deployment, and broader international commitments toward cleaner and more sustainable energy systems.

The attractiveness of photovoltaic systems lies not only in their environmental contribution, but also in their technical flexibility. PV systems are modular by nature, allowing them to be deployed at very different scales, from small residential rooftops to large utility-scale power plants. They operate silently, generate electricity without direct greenhouse gas emissions during operation, and contain no rotating mechanical components. As a result, they are less exposed to mechanical wear than many conventional generation systems and generally require limited maintenance. In utility-scale installations, operation and maintenance costs average around 13~USD/kW/year, compared with 20--93~USD/kW/year for onshore wind systems depending on country and configuration \citep{irena_costs2025}. In addition, well-maintained PV installations commonly achieve service lifetimes exceeding 25 years, making them attractive long-term energy assets.

This global momentum is particularly relevant for countries with strong solar resources. Algeria, due to its geographical location and high solar irradiation potential, is well positioned to benefit from photovoltaic energy. In recent years, the country has begun to integrate solar power more actively into its national energy strategy as part of broader efforts to diversify the energy mix and support long-term energy security. Sonelgaz currently operates 29 PV plants with a total installed capacity of 383.1~MW, producing 590.44~GWh in 2024. Moreover, 21 additional plants representing approximately 3,200~MWc are currently under construction \citep{energygov2025}. These investments reflect the growing role that solar energy is expected to play in the Algerian energy sector.

However, the success of photovoltaic deployment cannot be assessed through installed capacity alone. A PV plant fulfils its technical and economic purpose only if it continues to operate reliably throughout its expected lifetime. As the number and size of PV installations increase, ensuring high availability, stable production, and early detection of performance degradation becomes a central operational challenge. The issue is therefore not only to build PV plants, but also to keep them productive, reliable, and safe over time. In this context, fault detection and diagnosis are essential for protecting energy yield, reducing operational losses, and extending the useful lifetime of PV assets.

This operational concern frames the present thesis, which forms part of a national research programme (PNR) conducted at the Centre de Développement des Énergies Renouvelables (CDER), in collaboration with the \textit{Diagnostics} and \textit{Hybrid Systems} teams.

\section*{Problem Statement and Motivation}

Although photovoltaic systems are often considered reliable and relatively low-maintenance, their operation in real outdoor environments remains affected by various faults and degradation mechanisms. PV modules, strings, inverters, sensors, cables, and protection devices are continuously exposed to environmental stress, ageing, soiling, partial shading, electrical disturbances, and installation-related defects. These conditions can lead to abnormal operating behaviour and progressive performance losses.

One of the main difficulties in PV maintenance is that many faults are not directly visible. Unlike conventional generators, where mechanical failures may produce noise, vibration, or visible damage, PV faults often appear only through deviations in measurable quantities such as current, voltage, power, temperature, and irradiance-related performance indicators. A faulty string, a disconnected module, an inverter anomaly, or a shading-related mismatch may therefore remain unnoticed for long periods if the system is not continuously monitored.

The consequences of undetected faults can be significant. At the operational level, they reduce energy production and lower the performance ratio of the plant. At the economic level, they decrease revenue, increase maintenance costs, and may shorten equipment lifetime. In severe cases, electrical faults can also create safety hazards, including overheating, insulation failures, and arc-related risks. For utility-scale PV plants, even a small reduction in efficiency can lead to substantial energy losses when accumulated over months or years of operation.

Traditional maintenance strategies rely mainly on periodic inspections carried out by qualified technicians. While such approaches may be suitable for small residential systems, they become increasingly limited in large-scale PV plants. Modern utility installations may contain tens of thousands of modules distributed over large areas, making frequent manual inspection costly, time-consuming, and incomplete. Moreover, faults can occur between inspection periods and evolve before they are detected. This creates a gap between the timescale at which faults can affect the system and the frequency at which human inspection can identify them.

Automated Fault Detection and Diagnosis (FDD) systems offer a promising response to this challenge. By continuously analysing operational data, FDD methods can identify abnormal behaviour, support maintenance decisions, and improve the reliability of PV installations. In recent years, machine learning and deep learning techniques have attracted increasing attention for this purpose because of their ability to model complex relationships between environmental conditions, electrical variables, and fault signatures.

Despite this progress, important limitations remain in the existing literature. Many proposed approaches are developed and validated using simulated datasets or controlled experimental conditions, which may not fully capture the variability, noise, missing values, and imperfections of real field measurements. In addition, most studies focus primarily on classification accuracy, while giving less attention to the conditions required for practical implementation. These limitations restrict the transition of AI-based FDD from laboratory validation to operational use.

\noindent\textbf{Research problematic.}
\emph{How can photovoltaic fault diagnosis be designed to remain reliable under real operating conditions while satisfying the computational constraints required for embedded monitoring?}

\section*{Objectives}

The main objective of this thesis is to develop a reproducible and operationally relevant AI-based framework for fault detection and diagnosis in photovoltaic systems. More specifically, the thesis is structured around three main objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Develop a rigorous data pipeline.}  
    The first objective is to design and implement a complete workflow for real-world PV data processing. This includes raw data ingestion, temporal splitting, preprocessing, handling of missing or abnormal values, normalization, and feature engineering. Particular attention is given to preventing data leakage, ensuring that model evaluation reflects realistic operating conditions.

    \item \textbf{Design and evaluate lightweight FDD models.}  
    The second objective is to propose and compare efficient machine learning and deep learning models for two complementary diagnostic tasks: anomaly detection and fault classification. Anomaly detection determines whether the PV system is operating under normal or faulty conditions, while fault classification identifies the specific type of fault responsible for the observed deviation. The models are evaluated not only in terms of diagnostic performance, but also according to their suitability for implementation on resource-constrained hardware.

    \item \textbf{Demonstrate edge deployment.}
    The third objective is to deploy the validated FDD solution on a Jetson Nano embedded platform and integrate it with a graphical user interface for real-time monitoring. This step demonstrates the practical feasibility of the proposed approach beyond offline experimentation and highlights its potential for edge-based PV supervision. As part of this, the deployment includes a drift-monitoring layer that tracks whether operating conditions remain within the training regime and, if a sustained departure is detected, enables an operator-gated model update.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Part~I presents the theoretical and scientific background of the work. Chapter~\ref{ch:pv-systems-fdd} introduces the fundamentals of photovoltaic systems, presents the main fault categories, and reviews the artificial intelligence concepts relevant to PV fault detection and diagnosis. Chapter~\ref{ch:related-work} surveys existing data-driven FDD approaches, discusses available datasets, and identifies the research gaps addressed in this thesis. Part~II presents the main contributions, including the proposed data pipeline, feature engineering methodology, anomaly detection experiments, fault classification results, and embedded deployment on the Jetson Nano platform. Finally, the thesis concludes with a synthesis of the main findings, a discussion of limitations, and recommendations for future research.
