\section*{Context}

Over the past two decades, photovoltaic (PV) technology has established itself as one of the fastest-growing sources of electricity in the world. Record-breaking deployment figures have been recorded year after year, with global cumulative installed capacity reaching approximately 2,383 gigawatts by the end of 2025 and continuing to climb as module costs decline and national climate commitments intensify \citep{irena2026}. This expansion is not merely a market trend. It reflects a profound and necessary restructuring of global energy systems in response to the twin pressures of fossil fuel depletion and climate change. The 2015 Paris Agreement accelerated this shift by committing signatory nations to limit global warming to well below two degrees Celsius, and solar energy has since emerged as a central pillar of decarbonization strategies across virtually every region of the world.

Photovoltaic systems are particularly attractive within this context because of several intrinsic advantages that distinguish them from other renewable energy technologies. Their modular architecture allows deployment at scales ranging from a few panels on a residential rooftop to hundreds of megawatts in utility-scale power plants. They operate silently and produce no direct greenhouse gas emissions during generation. Because photovoltaic arrays contain no rotating machinery, they are free from the mechanical wear that drives maintenance costs in wind turbines and conventional thermal plants; the weighted average operational and maintenance cost for utility-scale PV was approximately 13 US dollars per kilowatt per year at the global level in 2024, compared to a range of 20 to 93 US dollars per kilowatt per year for onshore wind depending on the country and turbine configuration \citep{irena_costs2025}. Service lifetimes of twenty-five years or more are routinely achieved by well-maintained systems. These characteristics, combined with continuously improving conversion efficiencies and falling manufacturing costs, make solar photovoltaics a foundational technology for the energy transition.
\section*{Problematic and Motivation}

The economic promise of photovoltaic technology rests on a crucial premise: that installations will operate as designed over their projected service lifetime. In reality, field conditions expose PV systems to fault conditions that progressively degrade their performance. Unlike conventional generation technologies equipped with rotating machinery, which tend to fail in ways that are mechanically audible or visually apparent, photovoltaic faults are largely invisible to direct observation; their presence can only be inferred from deviations in the system's measurable physical quantities. When these faults go undetected, energy yield falls below expectations, equipment sustains damage that shortens its operational lifespan, and in the most severe cases, electrical anomalies create genuine safety hazards. Technical risk assessments of grid-connected PV plants have documented these losses quantitatively, finding that undetected faults account for a non-trivial fraction of lifetime energy underproduction and are a primary driver of unplanned maintenance expenditure \citep{herz2021}.

The conventional response to this challenge is periodic manual inspection by qualified technicians. At the scale of a residential system this approach remains practical, but at the scale of modern utility installations, which may encompass tens of thousands of modules distributed across hundreds of hectares, it becomes economically prohibitive and operationally insufficient. Inspection intervals measured in months are fundamentally mismatched with the timescale on which faults develop and cause damage, and no human inspector can feasibly monitor the continuous stream of measurements that a large plant generates. This gap between the cadence of human observation and the dynamics of fault development is the central operational challenge of PV maintenance at scale.

Automated Fault Detection and Diagnosis (FDD) addresses this gap by continuously monitoring the system's measurable physical quantities and automatically identifying deviations that signal a fault condition. Machine learning and deep learning methods have been extensively investigated for this purpose and have demonstrated promising detection and classification performance under controlled experimental conditions \citep{navid2021, elbanby2023}. However, critical limitations persist: existing approaches are predominantly validated on simulated rather than real-world data, and do not account for the constraints of embedded deployment. Addressing these challenges jointly requires a rigorous end-to-end data pipeline, experimentally sound validation methodology, and model architectures suited to the constraints of both the data and the deployment environment.

\section*{Objectives}

This thesis pursues three main objectives that together constitute a complete, reproducible, and operationally grounded AI-based FDD solution for photovoltaic systems. The first objective consists in developing a rigorous and reproducible data pipeline, spanning raw data ingestion, temporal splitting, preprocessing, and feature engineering, that is capable of handling the imperfections inherent in real-world PV measurements and that incorporates explicit mechanisms to prevent data leakage throughout the model development process. The second objective consists in proposing and systematically evaluating cost-effective, lightweight machine learning and deep learning architectures for two complementary diagnostic tasks: anomaly detection, which identifies whether a system is operating under fault conditions, and fault classification, which determines the specific fault type responsible for an observed deviation. These architectures are evaluated not only on their diagnostic accuracy but on their suitability for deployment under the computational constraints of embedded hardware. The third objective consists in deploying the validated solution on a Jetson Nano embedded platform and developing a graphical user interface for real-time system monitoring, thereby bridging the gap between laboratory model performance and operational field deployment and demonstrating the practical viability of the proposed approach at the edge.
