\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:related-work}

The field of fault detection and diagnosis in photovoltaic systems has attracted growing research interest over the past decade, driven by the rapid expansion of solar installations and the corresponding need for reliable automated monitoring solutions. This chapter reviews the existing literature with the objective of situating the contributions of this work within the current state of the art. The review is organized around three distinct families of diagnostic approaches, differentiated by the type of input data they exploit: thermographic imaging, current-voltage characteristic curve analysis, and operational electrical and meteorological measurements. Before examining these methodological families, it is necessary to characterize the datasets available in the field, as the nature of the training data fundamentally conditions the validity of performance claims and the generalizability of the proposed models. The chapter closes with a critical synthesis of the identified limitations and a statement of the research gaps that motivate the contributions of this thesis.

\section{Data Survey}
\label{sec:data-survey}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The availability of labeled, high-quality data is the foundational prerequisite for any supervised or semi-supervised learning approach to PV fault diagnosis. A comprehensive survey of open datasets for assessing PV system reliability highlights a striking asymmetry in the current data landscape: fault-labeled datasets collected from real field installations are rare, primarily because acquiring them requires either waiting for faults to occur naturally, a process that may span years, or organizing dedicated measurement campaigns in which fault conditions are artificially induced under controlled conditions \citep{chen2025datasets}. Simulated datasets, by contrast, can be readily reproduced from circuit models using tools such as MATLAB/Simulink and are consequently far more abundant in the literature, though their idealized statistical properties limit their relevance to real operational scenarios. The picture that emerges from this survey is one of significant data scarcity. The number of publicly available, fault-labeled datasets based on genuine field measurements remains extremely limited, and those that exist cover a narrow range of climatic conditions, PV technologies, and fault types. This scarcity has pushed a substantial fraction of the research community toward simulated data, which introduces a fundamental validity concern: models that achieve high diagnostic accuracy on numerically generated data may fail to generalize when confronted with the sensor imperfections, environmental variability, and installation-specific behavior of real PV systems. A further obstacle is the absence of any cross-dataset standardization: the available labeled datasets differ in their measurement channels, sampling frequencies, and fault taxonomies, making direct aggregation or multi-source fusion impractical without substantial domain adaptation effort.

\section{Thermal Image-Based Fault Detection}
\label{sec:thermal}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Infrared thermography has been widely adopted as a diagnostic modality in PV fault detection research, primarily for its ability to reveal hotspot anomalies that are invisible to conventional optical inspection. In a healthy PV module, the temperature distribution across the cell surface is approximately uniform under steady irradiance conditions. Faults such as shading, cell cracking, bypass diode failures, and soiling create localized thermal dissipation patterns that manifest as temperature differentials detectable by infrared cameras. The diagnostic pipeline for thermographic approaches typically involves acquiring aerial images of PV arrays using unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) equipped with thermal sensors, applying image preprocessing to normalize illumination and correct geometric distortions, and then feeding the processed images into a classification model.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have become the dominant model family for thermographic fault diagnosis, owing to their proven ability to extract spatially local features from image data. Early work framed the problem as a binary discrimination between healthy and faulty modules; \citet{manno2021thermal} applied convolutional feature extraction directly to thermographic images of PV modules, performing binary classification between operative and hotspot conditions on images collected from a real installation and achieving classification accuracy exceeding 95\%. Transfer learning was subsequently exploited to compensate for the scarcity of large labeled infrared datasets: \citet{akram2020transfer} pretrained a lightweight convolutional network on electroluminescence images and transferred its learned representation to infrared thermography for module defect detection, reaching 99.23\% accuracy. The release of the InfraredSolarModules benchmark, comprising 20{,}000 cropped module-level thermographs annotated across eleven anomaly categories, enabled the move from binary to fine-grained multi-class diagnosis; \citet{korkmaz2022multiscale} built a three-branch multi-scale CNN on top of an AlexNet backbone and reported an average accuracy of 97.32\% on the binary task and 93.51\% across the eleven anomaly classes of this benchmark. More recent work has shifted from patch classification toward object detection on full aerial frames, exploiting the natural acquisition format of UAV inspections. \citet{xie2024styolo} extended YOLOv8 with a spatial-channel reconstruction convolution module and a triplet attention mechanism, improving the mean Average Precision while reducing model size by 15\% relative to the YOLOv8s baseline on a self-collected UAV thermal dataset covering three defect types. In a similar direction, \citet{dhimish2025hotspot} introduced HOTSPOT-YOLO, a lightweight attention-driven architecture explicitly designed for the small and low-contrast thermal anomalies typical of drone-based PV inspections, reporting a mean Average Precision of 90.8\%.
Despite these results, thermographic approaches carry two fundamental limitations that restrict their applicability in large-scale operational deployment. The first is the cost and logistics of the acquisition infrastructure. Generating thermographic images at the resolution required for reliable hotspot detection requires calibrated infrared cameras, which are substantially more expensive than standard silicon-based sensors. When combined with UAVs capable of carrying such payloads and the flight operations required to systematically survey a utility-scale installation, the per-inspection cost becomes prohibitive for routine continuous monitoring. Industry data place the all-in cost of an infrared scan campaign, including UAV flight, image analysis, and reporting, in the range of 0.5 to 3.0 euros per module, while electroluminescence scans range from 3.0 to 10.0 euros per module \citep{herz2021}. For a utility-scale plant comprising tens of thousands of modules, a single inspection campaign therefore represents an outlay of tens to hundreds of thousands of euros, which is structurally incompatible with the cadence required for continuous fault awareness. The second limitation is the temporal resolution of the diagnostic process. Thermographic surveys are inherently event-driven and infrequent: a fault that develops between inspection campaigns may propagate for weeks or months before detection. These two constraints together mean that thermographic methods, despite their effectiveness within their domain of applicability, are not suitable as the primary monitoring modality for installations requiring near real-time fault awareness at low operational cost.

\section{Current-Voltage Curve-Based Fault Detection}
\label{sec:ivcurve}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The current-voltage (I-V) characteristic curve of a PV module encodes its complete electrical behavior at a given irradiance and temperature. Under healthy operating conditions, this curve follows a well-defined shape governed by the single-diode model: a plateau of quasi-constant current at low voltages, a knee region near the maximum power point, and a steep decline to zero at the open-circuit voltage. Different fault types produce characteristic distortions of this shape that serve as discriminative signatures. Partial shading introduces step-like discontinuities in the curve as successive bypass diodes activate. Increased series resistance tilts the slope of the knee region and reduces the fill factor. Decreased shunt resistance raises the slope of the plateau region near the short-circuit current. These predictable deformations have motivated a family of diagnostic approaches based on I-V curve measurement and analysis.

Machine learning methods applied to extracted I-V features have demonstrated consistently high performance in controlled settings. \citet{chen2017} proposed a kernel extreme learning machine trained on a set of features derived from the I-V curve, including the short-circuit current, the open-circuit voltage, the maximum power point coordinates, and the fill factor, reporting fault diagnosis accuracies above 98\% across multiple fault types on a simulated dataset. \citet{eskandari2020} applied an ensemble learning approach to line-to-line fault detection, using I-V and P-V curve features to distinguish between healthy operation and various fault configurations and achieving classification accuracy of 99\%. Deep learning approaches have extended this paradigm by bypassing manual feature extraction. \citet{liu2021sae} used a stacked autoencoder to learn a latent representation of the I-V curve directly and then applied clustering to group measurements into fault categories without requiring labeled training data. \citet{qu2024dust} proposed transforming I-V curves into graphical image representations and applying CNNs augmented with convolutional block attention modules (CBAM) to perform fault classification under varying soiling and dust conditions, achieving results above 99\% on both simulated and experimental data. \citet{gong2024deep} pushed this direction further by constructing a three-dimensional feature map from current, voltage, and power signals simultaneously, exploiting the fact that I-V and P-V curves exhibit distinct distortions under different fault conditions. The resulting multi-source information fusion network (MIFNet), optimized through an adaptive sparrow search algorithm, achieved an average recognition accuracy of 99.64\% across both single-component and multi-component fault scenarios.

The central limitation of I-V curve-based approaches lies in the nature of the measurement itself. Acquiring an I-V curve requires actively sweeping the operating point of the module or string across the full voltage range, which constitutes a discrete diagnostic event rather than a continuous observation of the system. Traditional sweep procedures rely on dedicated tracer instruments and require disconnection of the array, an operation that interrupts normal power generation; more recent inverter-integrated solutions, such as the Smart I-V Curve Diagnosis offered by Huawei, have considerably reduced this operational overhead by scanning an entire utility-scale plant in a matter of minutes from the inverter side, yet the procedure remains an event-driven sweep that briefly perturbs the maximum power point tracking and depends on vendor-specific smart inverter hardware. Beyond the procedural cost, the accuracy of any I-V sweep is sensitive to changes in irradiance and module temperature during the sweep duration, introducing uncertainty that can mask subtle fault signatures under variable cloud cover. These constraints position I-V curve analysis as a periodic diagnostic procedure rather than a continuous monitoring stream, and motivate the search for diagnostic methods that operate on the operational measurements already produced by standard plant instrumentation without imposing any additional sweep or operating-point disturbance.

\section{Electrical and Meteorological Data-Based Methods}
\label{sec:elec-meteo}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The third and most practically relevant family of data-driven PV fault detection methods operates on the operational electrical and meteorological measurements that modern PV installations continuously generate as part of their standard monitoring infrastructure. These measurements, including DC voltage and current at the string level, AC power at the inverter output, solar irradiance, and module and ambient temperatures, are already collected by dataloggers and SCADA systems in most grid-connected PV plants without any additional sensing hardware. This key advantage makes electrical and meteorological data-based approaches the most scalable option for continuous, near real-time fault monitoring.

\subsection{Machine Learning Approaches}

Classical machine learning methods were among the first to be applied to PV fault detection using operational electrical data, and they remain competitive with more recent deep learning approaches on tabular, low-frequency measurement data. \citet{garoudja2017ml} proposed a probabilistic neural network classifier for fault detection and diagnosis on the DC side of a grid-connected PV system, demonstrating robust performance under both noiseless and noisy measurement conditions and outperforming a conventional artificial neural network baseline. Support vector machines and boosted tree ensembles have been benchmarked side by side: \citet{suliman2024ml} compared multiple SVM kernel configurations against a Binary Adaptive XGBoost variant on a set of electrical fault scenarios including line-to-line, open-circuit, shading, and MPPT unit failures, with the boosted tree emerging as the strongest model at 87.56\% classification accuracy. Random forests have also been investigated in two-stage diagnostic pipelines: \citet{amiri2024rf} first extracted the parameters of the single-diode model using a modified grey wolf optimizer and then simulated the PV array in PSIM/MATLAB to produce labeled training data for two random forest classifiers, one for binary fault detection and one for multi-class fault diagnosis, reaching 99.4\% accuracy on both tasks and outperforming SVM, K-nearest neighbours, multi-layer perceptron, decision tree, and stochastic gradient descent baselines.

Gradient boosting algorithms have demonstrated remarkable effectiveness on tabular PV fault data. \citet{noura_explainable_2025} proposed a two-phase fault detection and diagnosis framework based on Extra Trees, achieving detection accuracy of 99.5\% and diagnosis accuracy of 98.7\% while integrating explainability through SHAP and LIME analyzers. \citet{kull_adaptive_2025} combined a Deep Belief Network for feature extraction with LightGBM for classification on a real-world SCADA dataset from a 100~MW solar facility, achieving 98.21\% accuracy on a seven-class problem. The study by \citet{zwirtes_fault_2025} is particularly relevant for generalization: the authors trained multiple ML classifiers on two real PV installations with different module characteristics and found that models trained on one system face significant performance degradation when applied to the other, underscoring the installation-specific nature of fault signatures. Unsupervised and semi-supervised approaches address the label scarcity problem that limits fully supervised methods; \citet{kabir_isolation_2023} applied Isolation Forest to PV operational data and demonstrated its effectiveness for detecting anomalous patterns without requiring labeled fault examples, while \citet{qu2022unsupervised} combined unsupervised weather pattern recognition with blending fitting models to enable label-free fault detection.

\subsection{Deep Learning Approaches}

Deep learning methods enable the automatic extraction of temporal and structural features from raw time-series measurements, reducing dependence on manual feature engineering. \citet{amiri2024dl} proposed a hybrid architecture combining one-dimensional convolutional feature extraction with a bidirectional gated recurrent unit for fault detection and diagnosis, demonstrating that the joint exploitation of convolutional and recurrent layers outperforms either component alone with near-perfect accuracy on their evaluation dataset. Autoencoder-based architectures have been used both as supervised feature extractors and as unsupervised anomaly detectors. \citet{seghiour2023autoencoder} combined a deep autoencoder for feature compression with a feed-forward neural network classifier for supervised multi-class fault diagnosis, taking the encoded representation of operating data as input to the classifier. \citet{titouni_hybrid_2025} extended the autoencoder principle with a hybrid CNN-Autoencoder architecture evaluated on the GPVS-Faults dataset, achieving 98.84\% test accuracy and outperforming several benchmark architectures. The Adaptive PolyKAN model of \citet{attouri_adaptive_2026} combined a polynomial Kolmogorov-Arnold network with an autoencoder-based dimensionality reduction strategy and was evaluated on both wind and solar power systems; on the photovoltaic dataset, the model reached a baseline classification accuracy of 95.61\% that rose to 96.47\% with the autoencoder compression enabled, while simultaneously reducing execution time by over 88\% and memory usage by 40\%.

Graph-based methods represent a more recent direction in this domain. \citet{yan_som-dbtgnet_2025} introduced SOM-DBTGNet, combining self-organizing maps for anomaly score generation with a dual-branch temporal graph convolution network that explicitly models topological relationships between measurement channels, achieving 90.7\% accuracy across six fault categories. Signal processing hybrid approaches further enrich the electrical modality. \citet{cai2022arc} applied variational mode decomposition to decompose string current signals into intrinsic mode functions before classifying them with a PSO-tuned SVM, enabling reliable detection of arc fault signatures invisible in raw time-domain measurements. Time-frequency transforms have been explored as alternative preprocessing front-ends for SCADA-based fault detection: \citet{saiprakash_improved_2024} extracted voltage and current features through the Stockwell transform and fed them to supervised classifiers, reporting 99.61\% accuracy on partial shading, open-circuit, and degradation faults.

Three patterns emerge from this synthesis. First, reported accuracies are consistently high across all modalities and method families, frequently in the 90 to 99\% band and occasionally approaching 100\%; these figures must nevertheless be interpreted with caution because a substantial fraction of the reviewed studies rely on circuit-level simulation rather than field measurements, or evaluate on small or artificially class-balanced test sets. Second, the electrical and meteorological modality is the most extensively represented in the literature, consistent with the observation that operational electrical measurements are the most readily available data type in real PV installations. Third, although several recent contributions explicitly pursue computationally efficient designs, ranging from the model-size reduction of ST-YOLO \citep{xie2024styolo} to the lightweight attention architecture of HOTSPOT-YOLO \citep{dhimish2025hotspot} and the 88\% execution-time and 40\% memory reduction reported for the PolyKAN-AE pipeline \citep{attouri_adaptive_2026}, efficiency in the reviewed literature is most often expressed through proxy metrics computed on workstation-class hardware, such as parameter count, floating-point operations, or theoretical inference time. End-to-end deployment on a representative embedded platform, with measured inference latency, memory footprint, and power consumption obtained on the target device under realistic operational conditions, has been reported only by a small number of works, notably the embedded monitoring system of \citet{costa2020sensors}.

\section{Research Gap and Motivation}
\label{sec:gap}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The preceding review, combined with the dataset landscape analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:data-survey}, identifies five interconnected gaps in the existing literature that motivate the specific contributions of this thesis.

\textbf{The operational profile of imaging-based and sweep-based modalities.} Thermographic approaches inspect modules through dedicated infrared cameras carried by unmanned aerial vehicles, with per-campaign costs in the range of 0.5 to 3.0 euros per module \citep{herz2021} and a cadence that is inherently event-driven rather than continuous. I-V curve methods rely on an active sweep of the module or string operating point; recent inverter-integrated solutions have considerably reduced the operational overhead of this sweep, but the procedure remains a discrete diagnostic event and depends on vendor-specific hardware. The operational electrical and meteorological measurements continuously produced by standard inverters and meteo sensors, by contrast, are already part of the instrumentation deployed for plant performance monitoring and do not require any fault-detection-specific hardware. This motivates anchoring the proposed approach on these continuously available channels, which together form the data type that any grid-connected installation already supplies.

\textbf{The under-representation of real-world data and the resulting generalization risk.} As Table~\ref{tab:synthesis} and the data survey of Section~\ref{sec:data-survey} jointly make visible, a substantial fraction of the published methods is trained and evaluated on circuit-level simulated data, which does not capture the sensor noise, missing samples, and weather-driven nonlinearity of real plants. \citet{zwirtes_fault_2025} provided direct evidence of this gap by showing that a classifier trained on one real PV system can fail substantially when applied to another with different module characteristics. The reported accuracy figures on clean simulated datasets are therefore an imperfect proxy for real-world performance, and the limited number of publicly available field-collected datasets restricts the empirical basis on which generalization claims can be made. This motivates building the entire experimental pipeline on real-world datasets, specifically the Costa dataset identified in Section~\ref{sec:data-survey}.

\textbf{Aggregate metrics that conceal the difficulty of subtle fault classes.} Many studies summarise their results through aggregate metrics, such as overall accuracy or weighted F1-score, which can be dominated by majority classes or by faults with clearly discriminative signatures. Faults with subtle electrical signatures, in particular the resistive degradation class, manifest as a gradual reduction in module current under conditions otherwise indistinguishable from normal operation and can be under-detected without this being apparent from the headline figure. Reconstruction-based anomaly detectors are particularly exposed to this risk, since they learn to reproduce the normal operating manifold and may reproduce mild degradation accurately enough to assign it a low anomaly score. This motivates a per-class evaluation discipline, with explicit attention to the most challenging fault categories, rather than reliance on aggregate accuracy alone.

\textbf{Measured embedded deployment under operational conditions.} The synthesis above shows that a growing number of contributions explicitly pursue lightweight model designs, but the validation of these designs on a representative embedded platform under realistic operational conditions has been reported by only a small number of works, notably the embedded monitoring system of \citet{costa2020sensors}. The vast majority of efficiency claims are expressed through proxy metrics computed on workstation-class hardware, such as parameter count or theoretical inference time, rather than through measurements obtained on the target device. This motivates a deployment effort that quantifies inference latency, memory footprint, and power consumption directly on a Jetson Nano platform, complementing the existing body of edge-deployed PV monitoring work.

\textbf{Reproducibility and lifecycle infrastructure.} The reviewed literature largely focuses on the diagnostic model itself rather than on the engineering scaffolding required to operate that model over time, such as versioned data, reproducible experiment tracking, and mechanisms to detect distributional drift between training and operational conditions. PV system behaviour evolves with module ageing, seasonal soiling patterns, and weather regime changes, all of which can degrade model performance silently if no monitoring mechanism is in place. This motivates an end-to-end pipeline built on standard MLOps tools, with data and pipeline versioning, structured experiment tracking, and the foundations for monitoring distributional drift between training and inference time.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Part~I has established the conceptual foundations of this work. Chapter~\ref{ch:pv-systems-fdd} introduced the operating principles of photovoltaic systems and the taxonomy of faults that affect them, while Section~\ref{sec:ai-fdd} reviewed the machine learning and deep learning techniques relevant to fault detection and diagnosis. The present chapter surveyed the data landscape and the three principal families of data-driven detection and diagnosis methods before articulating five research gaps. Together, these chapters justify the central design choices that follow: anchoring the work on operational electrical and meteorological measurements, building on the Costa real-world dataset, and pursuing measured embedded validation. Part~II presents the data pipeline, the detection and diagnosis models, and the embedded deployment that operationalise these choices.
